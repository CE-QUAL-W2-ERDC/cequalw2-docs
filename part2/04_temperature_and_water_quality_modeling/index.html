    Temperature and Water Quality Modeling¶

    This chapter describes the theoretical basis for temperature and water quality modeling in CE-QUAL-W2. The model simulates temperature and an arbitrary number of water quality constituents using the turbulent advection-diffusion equation. Each constituent is subject to lateral and vertical transport as well as kinetic source and sink terms that represent biological, chemical, and physical transformations.

    Turbulent Advection-Diffusion Equation¶

    Temperature and Water Quality Transport¶

    The laterally averaged transport equation for a constituent concentration \(\Phi\) is:

    \[ \frac{\partial B\Phi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial UB\Phi}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial WB\Phi}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(BD_x\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(BD_z\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial z}\right) = q_\Phi B + S_K B \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi\) = laterally averaged constituent concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(U\) = longitudinal, laterally averaged velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(W\) = vertical, laterally averaged velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(B\) = channel width, m
    • \(D_x\) = longitudinal temperature and constituent dispersion coefficient, m² s⁻¹
    • \(D_z\) = vertical temperature and constituent dispersion coefficient, m² s⁻¹
    • \(q_\Phi\) = lateral inflow or outflow mass flow rate of constituent per unit volume, g m⁻³ s⁻¹
    • \(S_K\) = kinetic source/sink term for constituent concentration, g m⁻³ s⁻¹

    The left-hand side of the equation represents the time rate of change, longitudinal advection, vertical advection, longitudinal dispersion, and vertical dispersion. The right-hand side includes lateral sources/sinks and internal kinetic transformations.

    Determination of \(D_z\) and \(D_x\)¶

    The vertical dispersion coefficient \(D_z\) is derived from the turbulence closure model described in Chapter 2. For temperature simulations, \(D_z\) is computed from the turbulent eddy viscosity and the turbulent Prandtl number.

    The longitudinal dispersion coefficient \(D_x\) accounts for the effects of differential advection that are not resolved by the laterally averaged model. The user specifies \(D_x\) through the control file parameter [DX]. Typical values range from 0 to 100 m² s⁻¹, though in many applications \(D_x\) is set to zero because numerical dispersion from the transport algorithm often exceeds the physical longitudinal dispersion.

    Temperature Auxiliary Functions¶

    Heat Exchange¶

    Temperature is modeled as a constituent with kinetic source/sink terms representing surface heat exchange and sediment heat exchange. The surface heat exchange rate is computed as:

    \[ S_T = \frac{H_{net}}{\rho C_p \Delta z} \]

    where:

    • \(H_{net}\) = net heat flux at the water surface, W m⁻²
    • \(\rho\) = water density, kg m⁻³
    • \(C_p\) = specific heat of water, J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹
    • \(\Delta z\) = surface layer thickness, m

    Surface Heat Exchange¶

    The net surface heat flux is the sum of several components:

    \[ H_{net} = H_s + H_a + H_{sr} + H_{ar} + H_e + H_c + H_{br} \]

    where:

    • \(H_s\) = incident short-wave solar radiation, W m⁻²
    • \(H_a\) = incident long-wave atmospheric radiation, W m⁻²
    • \(H_{sr}\) = reflected short-wave solar radiation, W m⁻²
    • \(H_{ar}\) = reflected long-wave atmospheric radiation, W m⁻²
    • \(H_e\) = evaporative heat loss, W m⁻²
    • \(H_c\) = conductive heat transfer, W m⁻²
    • \(H_{br}\) = back radiation (long-wave radiation emitted by the water surface), W m⁻²

    The model provides two approaches for computing surface heat exchange:

    • Term-by-term (TERM): Each heat flux component is calculated individually from meteorological data. Set SLHTC to TERM.
    • Equilibrium temperature (ET): The net heat exchange is computed as a linearized function of the difference between the water surface temperature and an equilibrium temperature. Set SLHTC to ET.

    Evaporation Models¶

    CE-QUAL-W2 includes multiple formulations for the wind speed function used in evaporative heat loss calculations. The evaporative heat loss is computed as:

    \[ H_e = f(W)(e_s - e_a) \]

    where:

    • \(f(W)\) = wind speed function, W m⁻² mmHg⁻¹
    • \(e_s\) = saturation vapor pressure at the water surface temperature, mmHg
    • \(e_a\) = vapor pressure of the overlying atmosphere, mmHg

    The user selects the evaporation formulation through the control file. Several wind speed function formulations are available including those from Brady, Harbeck, and Ryan-Harleman, among others.

    Equilibrium Temperature¶

    The equilibrium temperature approach computes the net heat flux as:

    \[ H_{net} = K_{eq}(T_{eq} - T_s) \]

    where:

    • \(K_{eq}\) = coefficient of surface heat exchange, W m⁻² °C⁻¹
    • \(T_{eq}\) = equilibrium temperature, °C
    • \(T_s\) = water surface temperature, °C

    The equilibrium temperature is the temperature at which the net heat flux is zero. Both \(K_{eq}\) and \(T_{eq}\) are computed internally by the model from meteorological data.

    Sediment Heat Exchange¶

    Sediment-water heat exchange is computed as:

    \[ H_{sed} = K_{sed}(T_{sed} - T) \]

    where:

    • \(K_{sed}\) = sediment-water heat exchange coefficient, W m⁻² °C⁻¹
    • \(T_{sed}\) = sediment temperature, °C
    • \(T\) = water temperature of the bottom layer, °C

    The sediment temperature is specified by the user through the control file parameter [TSED].

    Dynamic Shading¶

    CE-QUAL-W2 includes algorithms for computing the reduction of incident solar radiation due to topographic and vegetative shading. These algorithms account for the solar position (altitude and azimuth), local topography, and riparian vegetation along the channel.

    Solar Altitude and Azimuth¶

    The solar altitude angle \(\alpha_s\) and azimuth angle \(A_s\) are computed from standard astronomical relationships as functions of latitude, longitude, day of the year, and time of day.

    The solar altitude is computed as:

    \[ \sin(\alpha_s) = \sin(\phi)\sin(\delta) + \cos(\phi)\cos(\delta)\cos(h) \]

    where:

    • \(\phi\) = latitude
    • \(\delta\) = solar declination
    • \(h\) = hour angle

    Topographic Shading¶

    Topographic shading occurs when surrounding terrain blocks direct solar radiation from reaching the water surface. For each model segment, the user specifies topographic shade angles for both the left and right banks. When the solar altitude is less than the topographic shade angle, direct solar radiation is blocked.

    Vegetative Shading¶

    Vegetative shading accounts for the attenuation of solar radiation by riparian vegetation. The model computes the fraction of the water surface that is shaded based on tree height, crown width, distance from the bank, and solar position.

    Data Requirements¶

    Dynamic shading requires segment-specific data including:

    • Topographic shade angles (left and right bank)
    • Vegetation characteristics: tree height, crown width, distance from bank, and canopy density
    • Model segment orientation (azimuth)

    These data are specified in the shade input file.

    Ice Cover¶

    CE-QUAL-W2 simulates ice formation, growth, and decay. The ice model accounts for heat exchange between the atmosphere and ice surface, solar radiation transmission through ice, and heat exchange at the ice-water interface.

    Initial Formation¶

    Ice begins to form when the surface water temperature drops below the freezing temperature. The initial ice thickness is computed from the heat balance at the water surface.

    Air-Ice Flux Boundary Condition¶

    When ice is present, the surface heat exchange is computed between the atmosphere and the ice surface rather than the water surface. The heat flux through the ice is:

    \[ H_{ice} = \frac{k_{ice}}{h_{ice}}(T_1 - T_{ice}) \]

    where:

    • \(k_{ice}\) = thermal conductivity of ice, W m⁻¹ °C⁻¹
    • \(h_{ice}\) = ice thickness, m
    • \(T_1\) = temperature at the bottom of the ice, °C
    • \(T_{ice}\) = temperature at the top of the ice surface, °C

    Solar Radiation Under Ice¶

    Solar radiation penetration through ice is attenuated exponentially:

    \[ I(z) = (1 - \alpha_{ice})I_0 e^{-\lambda_{ice} h_{ice}} \]

    where:

    • \(\alpha_{ice}\) = ice albedo
    • \(I_0\) = incident solar radiation at the ice surface, W m⁻²
    • \(\lambda_{ice}\) = extinction coefficient for ice, m⁻¹
    • \(h_{ice}\) = ice thickness, m

    Ice Melt¶

    Ice melts when the heat flux from the water to the ice exceeds the heat flux from the ice to the atmosphere. Both surface melting (due to atmospheric heat flux) and bottom melting (due to water-to-ice heat flux) are computed.

    Ice-Water Flux Boundary Condition¶

    The heat exchange at the ice-water interface is computed using a bulk transfer coefficient:

    \[ H_{iw} = C_{iw}\rho C_p(T_w - T_f) \]

    where:

    • \(C_{iw}\) = ice-water heat exchange coefficient, m s⁻¹
    • \(T_w\) = water temperature below the ice, °C
    • \(T_f\) = freezing temperature, °C

    Freezing Temperature¶

    The freezing temperature depends on total dissolved solids (TDS) or salinity. For freshwater, the freezing temperature is approximately 0 °C. For saline water, the freezing temperature decreases with increasing salinity.

    Gain or Loss of Water¶

    When ice forms, water is removed from the computational domain. When ice melts, water (at the freezing temperature) is returned to the surface layer. The model accounts for these changes in water volume.

    Overview of Kinetic Source/Sink Term¶

    The kinetic source/sink term \(S_K\) in the transport equation represents the net effect of all internal biological, chemical, and physical transformations for each constituent. The following sections describe the source/sink formulations for each constituent modeled in CE-QUAL-W2.

    The model can simulate any combination of the following constituent groups:

    • Generic constituents (conservative tracers, volatile substances)
    • Bacteria
    • Dissolved gases (N₂, total dissolved gas)
    • Water age or residence time
    • CH₄, H₂S, SO₄
    • Fe²⁺, FeOOH, Mn²⁺, MnO₂
    • Inorganic suspended solids (ISS)
    • Total dissolved solids (TDS) or salinity
    • Dissolved organic matter (labile DOM, refractory DOM)
    • Particulate organic matter (labile POM, refractory POM)
    • Organic matter variable stoichiometry (LDOM-P, RDOM-P, LPOM-P, RPOM-P, LDOM-N, RDOM-N, LPOM-N, RPOM-N)
    • Organic carbon constituents (LPOC, RPOC, LDOC, RDOC)
    • Carbonaceous biochemical oxygen demand (CBOD, CBODP, CBODN)
    • Algae (multiple groups with vertical migration and toxin production)
    • Periphyton/Epiphyton
    • Macrophytes
    • Zooplankton
    • Phosphorus (PO₄)
    • Ammonium (NH₄)
    • Nitrate-Nitrite (NO₃ + NO₂)
    • Dissolved silica, Particulate biogenic silica
    • Dissolved oxygen (including reaeration)
    • Organic sediments (zero-order and first-order models)
    • Sediment variable stoichiometry (P, N, C)
    • Sediment diagenesis model
    • Total inorganic carbon, alkalinity, pH, and carbonate species
    • Temperature rate multipliers

    Generic Constituent¶

    Conservative Tracer¶

    A conservative tracer has no internal sources or sinks. The source/sink term is zero:

    \[ S_{tracer} = 0 \]

    Conservative tracers are useful for tracking water masses from specific inflow sources such as tributaries, point sources, or nonpoint sources.

    Volatile Generic Constituent¶

    For a volatile generic constituent, losses occur through gas transfer at the water surface:

    \[ S_{volatile} = -K_v(\Phi - \Phi_{sat}) \]

    where:

    • \(K_v\) = volatilization rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi\) = constituent concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{sat}\) = saturation concentration, g m⁻³

    Bacteria¶

    Bacteria are modeled with first-order decay and photodegradation. The source/sink term is:

    \[ S_{bact} = -K_d \Phi_{bact} - K_{photo} \Phi_{bact} \]

    where:

    • \(K_d\) = first-order decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{photo}\) = photodegradation rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{bact}\) = bacteria concentration, counts mL⁻¹ or equivalent

    Dissolved Nitrogen Gas and Total Dissolved Gas¶

    N₂ Dissolved Gas¶

    Dissolved nitrogen gas (N₂) is modeled as a generic constituent with gas transfer at the water surface. The source/sink term includes reaeration:

    \[ S_{N_2} = \frac{A_{sur}}{V_{sur}} K_L(\Phi_{N_2sat} - \Phi_{N_2}) \]

    where:

    • \(K_L\) = gas transfer velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{N_2sat}\) = N₂ saturation concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{N_2}\) = dissolved N₂ concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(A_{sur}\) = surface area, m²
    • \(V_{sur}\) = surface layer volume, m³

    N₂ saturation is computed from temperature and atmospheric pressure using standard solubility equations.

    Total Dissolved Gas¶

    Total dissolved gas (TDG) pressure is the sum of the partial pressures of all dissolved gases (primarily N₂ and O₂). TDG is computed as a derived quantity from dissolved N₂ and dissolved O₂ concentrations.

    Water Age or Residence Time¶

    Water age (or residence time) is a constituent that increases linearly with time:

    \[ S_{age} = 1 \]

    The concentration represents the elapsed time since water entered the model domain from a specific source. Water age is useful for understanding transport time scales and mixing patterns.

    Dissolved Gas Pressure¶

    Dissolved gas pressure is computed from the concentrations of dissolved N₂ and dissolved O₂ using Henry's Law relationships and is reported as a percentage of atmospheric pressure (percent saturation).

    Water Column CH₄, H₂S, SO₄¶

    Methane (CH₄)¶

    Methane in the water column is produced by sediment diagenesis under anaerobic conditions and is oxidized in the presence of dissolved oxygen. The source/sink term for CH₄ is:

    \[ S_{CH_4} = -K_{CH_4}\Phi_{CH_4} \]

    where:

    • \(K_{CH_4}\) = CH₄ oxidation rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{CH_4}\) = CH₄ concentration, g m⁻³

    CH₄ oxidation consumes dissolved oxygen according to the stoichiometric relationship.

    Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S)¶

    H₂S is produced in the sediments under anaerobic conditions through sulfate reduction and is oxidized in the water column. The source/sink term for H₂S is:

    \[ S_{H_2S} = -K_{H_2S}\Phi_{H_2S} \]

    where:

    • \(K_{H_2S}\) = H₂S oxidation rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{H_2S}\) = H₂S concentration, g m⁻³

    Sulfate (SO₄)¶

    SO₄ is consumed through sulfate reduction under anaerobic conditions in the sediments and water column. The source/sink term for SO₄ includes the sulfate reduction rate.

    Water Column Fe²⁺, FeOOH, Mn²⁺, MnO₂¶

    Ferrous Iron Fe²⁺¶

    The rate equation in the water column for Fe(II) is:

    \[ S_{Fe(II)} = -\underbrace{k_{Fe(II)0}\Phi_{DO} 10^{2(pH-7)} \Phi_{Fe(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} + \underbrace{k_{FeOOH}\left(\frac{K_{FeOOH}}{K_{FeOOH} + \Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{FeOOH}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    where:

    • \(k_{FeOOH}\) = reduction rate in water column, s⁻¹
    • \(k_{Fe(II)0}\) = oxidation rate in water column, s⁻¹ (g m⁻³)⁻¹
    • \(K_{FeOOH}\) = half-saturation constant for oxygen for this reaction, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{DO}\) = dissolved oxygen concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{Fe(II)}\) = ferrous iron concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{FeOOH}\) = iron oxyhydroxide concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(pH\) = pH in water column

    Iron Oxyhydroxide FeOOH¶

    The rate equation in the water column for iron oxyhydroxide is:

    \[ S_{FeOOH} = \underbrace{k_{Fe(II)0}\Phi_{DO} 10^{2(pH-7)} \Phi_{Fe(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} - \underbrace{k_{FeOOH}\left(\frac{K_{FeOOH}}{K_{FeOOH} + \Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{FeOOH}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    Mn²⁺ (Divalent Manganese)¶

    The rate equation for Mn(II) in the water column is:

    \[ S_{Mn(II)} = -\underbrace{k_{Mn(II)0}\Phi_{DO} 10^{2(pH-7)} \Phi_{Mn(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} + \underbrace{k_{MnO_2}\left(\frac{K_{MnO_2}}{K_{MnO_2} + \Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{MnO_2}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    where:

    • \(k_{MnO_2}\) = reduction rate in water column, s⁻¹
    • \(k_{Mn(II)0}\) = oxidation rate in water column, s⁻¹ (g m⁻³)⁻¹
    • \(K_{MnO_2}\) = half-saturation constant for oxygen for this reaction, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{Mn(II)}\) = Mn(II) concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{MnO_2}\) = manganese dioxide concentration, g m⁻³

    Manganese Dioxide MnO₂¶

    The rate equation in the water column for manganese dioxide is:

    \[ S_{MnO_2} = \underbrace{k_{Mn(II)0}\Phi_{DO} 10^{2(pH-7)} \Phi_{Mn(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} - \underbrace{k_{MnO_2}\left(\frac{K_{MnO_2}}{K_{MnO_2} + \Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{MnO_2}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    Inorganic Suspended Solids¶

    Inorganic suspended solids (ISS) are modeled as settling particles with no internal kinetic reactions other than settling:

    \[ S_{ISS} = -\omega_{ISS}\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A \Phi_{ISS}}{\partial z} \]

    where:

    • \(\omega_{ISS}\) = inorganic suspended solids settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{ISS}\) = ISS concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(A\) = horizontal area, m²

    Multiple ISS groups can be defined with different settling velocities and particle sizes. When ISS settles to the bottom, it is lost from the water column. Unlike organic sediments, ISS does not accumulate in the sediment compartment.

    Note

    In the sediment diagenesis model for Version 4.22 and earlier, there was a group for Mature Fine Tailings that was added as a generic constituent. The current model now sets this state variable as a suspended solids group.

    Total Dissolved Solids or Salinity¶

    Total dissolved solids (TDS) affect water density and ionic strength, thereby affecting water movements, pH, and the distribution of carbonate species. Dissolved solids are normally expressed as TDS in freshwater applications. Estuarine applications normally use salinity.

    Either TDS or salinity can be used with the choice indicated by the parameter [WTYPEC] specified in the control file. If TDS is used, the units are g m⁻³; if salinity is used, the units are kg m⁻³. TDS and salinity are not necessarily equivalent. In the model, both are treated conservatively with the rate term set to zero.

    Labile DOM¶

    Because of the importance of dissolved oxygen in aquatic systems, all constituents exerting an oxygen demand must be included in kinetic formulations. This demand is often measured as the biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), which includes microbial respiration and metabolism of various organic and inorganic compounds. Dissolved organic matter (DOM) is composed of labile and refractory components. DOM is modeled as two separate compartments because of the different decomposition rates of the two groups.

    The rate equation for labile DOM is:

    \[ S_{LDOM} = \underbrace{\sum K_{ae}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal excretion}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{am})K_{am}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{ee}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton excretion}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{em})K_{em}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{LDOM}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\text{labile DOM decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM decay}}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{mm})K_{mm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{macrophyte mortality/excretion}} \tag{4-70} \]

    where:

    • \(P_{am}\) = pattern coefficient for algal mortality
    • \(P_{em}\) = pattern coefficient for epiphyton mortality
    • \(P_{mm}\) = partitioning coefficient for macrophyte mortality
    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = temperature rate multiplier for organic matter decay
    • \(K_{ae}\) = algal excretion rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{am}\) = algal mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ee}\) = epiphyton excretion rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{em}\) = epiphyton mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{mm}\) = macrophyte mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{LDOM}\) = labile DOM decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{L \to R}\) = labile to refractory DOM transfer rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_a\) = algal concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_e\) = epiphyton concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{LDOM}\) = labile DOM concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{macro}\) = macrophyte concentration, g m⁻³

    Refractory DOM¶

    Refractory DOM is composed of compounds in the aquatic environment that slowly decompose, exerting oxygen demand over long periods. Internally, refractory DOM is produced from the decomposition of labile DOM.

    The rate equation for refractory DOM is:

    \[ S_{RDOM} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RDOM}\Phi_{RDOM}}_{\text{decay}} \tag{4-71} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = temperature rate multiplier
    • \(K_{RDOM}\) = refractory DOM decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{L \to R}\) = transfer rate from labile DOM, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{LDOM}\) = labile DOM concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{RDOM}\) = refractory DOM concentration, g m⁻³

    The rate terms are evaluated in subroutine REFRACTORY_DOM.

    Labile POM¶

    Labile particulate organic matter (LPOM) represents particulate organic material in the water column. Decaying particulate organic matter is a source of refractory particulate organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, and inorganic carbon. A stoichiometric relationship governs mineralization of ammonium, phosphorus, and inorganic carbon, and an oxygen demand is exerted as LPOM decomposes.

    When LPOM settles to the bottom, it accumulates and decays in the sediment compartment or the sediment diagenesis compartment if activated for the simulation.

    The rate equation for LPOM is:

    \[ S_{LPOM} = \underbrace{\sum P_{am}K_{am}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{em}K_{em}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} - \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\omega_{POM}\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A\Phi_{LPOM}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{mm}K_{mm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zm}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton mortality}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}(1-Z_{effic})\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton excretion}} - \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}\left(\frac{\sigma_{pom}}{\sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo} + \sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{LPOM}}\right)\Phi_{LPOM}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton ingestion of LPOM}} \tag{4-72} \]

    where:

    • \(P_{am}\) = partition coefficient for algal mortality
    • \(P_{em}\) = partition coefficient for epiphyton mortality
    • \(P_{mm}\) = partition coefficient for macrophyte mortality
    • \(P_{zooP}\) = partition coefficient for zooplankton LPOM grazing
    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = temperature rate multiplier for organic matter
    • \(\omega_{POM}\) = POM settling rate, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{am}\) = algal mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{em}\) = epiphyton mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{mm}\) = macrophyte mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{zm}\) = zooplankton mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{zg}\) = zooplankton growth rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{LPOM}\) = labile POM decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{effic}\) = zooplankton ingestion efficiency
    • \(K_{L \to R}\) = transfer rate from labile POM to refractory POM, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_a\) = algal concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_e\) = periphyton/epiphyton concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{macro}\) = macrophyte concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{LPOM}\) = detritus concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{zoo}\) = zooplankton concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\sigma_{pom}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for particulate organic matter
    • \(\sigma_{alg}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for algae
    • \(\sigma_{zoo}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for zooplankton

    POM settling and accumulation in the sediment compartment is handled identically to the algal compartment.

    Refractory POM¶

    Refractory POM is slowly decaying non-living, organic matter that settles. The source/sink terms are first-order decay, the conversion of LPOM to RPOM, and sedimentation. The settled organic matter becomes part of the first-order sediment model or the sediment diagenesis model if they are active.

    The rate equation for RPOM is:

    \[ S_{RPOM} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RPOM}\Phi_{RPOM}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{\omega_{RPOM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{RPOM}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-73} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = temperature rate multiplier
    • \(K_{L \to R}\) = transfer rate from labile POM to refractory POM, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{RPOM}\) = refractory POM decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_{POM}\) = POM settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{LPOM}\) = labile POM concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{RPOM}\) = refractory POM concentration, g m⁻³

    Organic Matter Variable Stoichiometry¶

    CE-QUAL-W2 includes variable stoichiometry of organic matter starting with Version 3.5. Previous versions used fixed stoichiometric constants for the ratios of nitrogen and phosphorus to organic matter.

    Eight constituents are required to simulate the amount of nitrogen and phosphorus in labile dissolved organic matter (LDOM), refractory organic matter (RDOM), labile particulate organic matter (LPOM), and refractory particulate organic matter (RPOM). These constituents are summarized in the following table. All inputs of organic matter accumulate N and P according to the stoichiometry of the incoming organic matter.

    Abbreviation Constituent
    LDOM-P Labile dissolved organic matter phosphorus
    RDOM-P Refractory dissolved organic matter phosphorus
    LPOM-P Labile particulate organic matter phosphorus
    RPOM-P Refractory particulate organic matter phosphorus
    LDOM-N Labile dissolved organic matter nitrogen
    RDOM-N Refractory dissolved organic matter nitrogen
    LPOM-N Labile particulate organic matter nitrogen
    RPOM-N Refractory particulate organic matter nitrogen

    LDOM-P¶

    LDOM-P is the amount of phosphorus in labile dissolved organic matter. The rate equation for LDOM-P is:

    \[ S_{\text{LDOM-P}} = \underbrace{\sum K_{ae}\delta_{Pa}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal excretion}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{am})K_{am}\delta_{Pa}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{ee}\delta_{Pe}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton excretion}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{em})K_{em}\delta_{Pe}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{mm})K_{mm}\delta_{Pm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} - \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM-P}}_{\text{labile DOM-P decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM-P}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM-P decay}}} \tag{4-74} \]

    RDOM-P¶

    The rate equation for RDOM-P is:

    \[ S_{RDOM-P} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LDOM-P}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RDOM}\Phi_{RDOM-P}}_{\text{decay}} \tag{4-75} \]

    LPOM-P¶

    The rate equation for LPOM-P is:

    \[ S_{LPOM-P} = \underbrace{\sum P_{am}K_{am}\delta_{Pa}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{em}K_{em}\delta_{Pe}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{mm}P_{mpom}K_{mm}\delta_{Pm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zm}\delta_{Pz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton mortality}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM-P}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM-P}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}(1-Z_{effic})\delta_{Pz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton excretion}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}\frac{\Phi_{LPOM-P}}{\Phi_{LPOM}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{pom}\Phi_{LPOM}}{\sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo} + \sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{LPOM}}\right)\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton ingestion}} - \underbrace{\omega_{POM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{LPOM-P}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-76} \]

    RPOM-P¶

    The rate equation for RPOM-P is:

    \[ S_{RPOM} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM-P}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RPOM}\Phi_{RPOM-P}}_{\text{decay}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{mm}(1-P_{mpom})K_{mm}\delta_{Pm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} - \underbrace{\omega_{POM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{RPOM-P}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-77} \]

    LDOM-N¶

    LDOM-N is the amount of nitrogen in labile dissolved organic matter. The rate equation for LDOM-N is:

    \[ S_{\text{LDOM-N}} = \underbrace{\sum K_{ae}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal excretion}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{am})K_{am}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{ee}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton excretion}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{em})K_{em}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum(1-P_{mm})K_{mm}\delta_{Nm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} - \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM-N}}_{\text{labile DOM-N decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM-N}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM-N decay}}} \tag{4-78} \]

    RDOM-N¶

    The rate equation for RDOM-N is:

    \[ S_{RDOM-N} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LDOM-N}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RDOM}\Phi_{RDOM-N}}_{\text{decay}} \tag{4-79} \]

    LPOM-N¶

    The rate equation for LPOM-N is:

    \[ S_{LPOM-N} = \underbrace{\sum P_{am}K_{am}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{em}K_{em}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{mm}P_{mpom}K_{mm}\delta_{Nm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} + \underbrace{K_{zm}\delta_{Nz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton mortality}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM-N}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM-N}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}(1-Z_{effic})\delta_{Nz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton excretion}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum K_{zg}\frac{\Phi_{LPOM-N}}{\Phi_{LPOM}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{pom}\Phi_{LPOM}}{\sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo} + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{LPOM}}\right)\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton ingestion}} - \underbrace{\omega_{POM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{LPOM-N}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-80} \]

    RPOM-N¶

    The rate equation for RPOM-N is:

    \[ S_{RPOM} = \underbrace{K_{L \to R}\Phi_{LPOM-N}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POM decay}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{RPOM}\Phi_{RPOM-N}}_{\text{decay}} + \underbrace{\sum P_{mm}(1-P_{mpom})K_{mm}\delta_{Nm}\Phi_m}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} - \underbrace{\omega_{POM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{RPOM-N}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-81} \]

    Organic Carbon Constituents¶

    The organic matter state variables (LPOM, RPOM, RDOM, LDOM) are based on organic matter dry weight. CE-QUAL-W2 also supports organic matter expressed as carbon (C). Many other models use this approach because organic matter is rarely measured in the field, whereas organic carbon is routinely measured. Instead of LDOM, LPOM, RDOM, and RPOM, the user can specify LDOC, LPOC, RDOC, and RPOC.

    Organic matter is difficult for laboratories to measure directly. However, collective water quality parameters such as organic carbon, organic nitrogen, and organic phosphorus are commonly measured. The model includes an option for directly simulating organic carbon constituents without organic matter. The four organic carbon state variables are labile dissolved organic carbon (LDOC), refractory dissolved organic carbon (RDOC), labile particulate organic carbon (LPOC), and refractory particulate organic carbon (RPOC).

    LPOC¶

    The kinetic source/sink term for LPOC is:

    \[ S_{LPOC} = \underbrace{\sum K_{am}\delta_{ca}\Phi_a P_{am}}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{em}\delta_{ce}\Phi_e P_{em}}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} - \underbrace{\omega_{LPOM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{LPOC}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LPOC}\Phi_{LPOC}}_{\text{LPOC decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LPOC \to RPOC}\Phi_{LPOC}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POC transfer}}} + \underbrace{\gamma_{OM}K_{LPOM \to LDOM}\Phi_{LPOC}}_{\text{LPOC hydrolysis}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{mm}\delta_{cm}\Phi_{macro}P_{nom}P_{mmLPOM}}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} + \underbrace{K_{zm}\delta_{cz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton mortality}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum\left(Z_u\delta_{cz}\Phi_{zoo}\frac{\Phi_{LPOM}}{\sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \Phi_{LPOM} + \sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo}}\right)}_{\text{zooplankton growth}} \tag{4-82} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{LPOC}\) = LPOC concentration, g C m⁻³
    • \(P_{am}\) = fraction of algal mortality into LPOM (0--1)
    • \(P_{em}\) = fraction of epiphyton mortality into LPOM (0--1)
    • \(P_{mm}\) = fraction of macrophyte mortality into LPOM (0--1)
    • \(\delta_{ca}\) = stoichiometric ratio of carbon in algae, g C (g DW)⁻¹
    • \(\delta_{ce}\) = stoichiometric ratio of carbon in epiphyton, g C (g DW)⁻¹
    • \(\delta_{cm}\) = stoichiometric ratio of carbon in macrophyte, g C (g DW)⁻¹
    • \(\delta_{cz}\) = stoichiometric ratio of carbon in zooplankton, g C (g DW)⁻¹
    • \(K_{LPOC}\) = LPOC decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{LPOC \to RPOC}\) = transfer rate from LPOC to RPOC, s⁻¹

    RPOC¶

    The kinetic source/sink term for RPOC is:

    \[ S_{RPOC} = \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LPOC \to RPOC}\Phi_{LPOC}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{POC transfer}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{RPOC}\Phi_{RPOC}}_{\text{RPOC decay}} - \underbrace{\omega_{RPOM}\frac{\partial \Phi_{RPOC}}{\partial z}}_{\text{RPOC settling}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{RPOM \to LDOM}\Phi_{RPOC}}_{\text{RPOC hydrolysis}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{mm}\delta_{cm}\Phi_{macro}P_{mm}(1-P_{mmLPOM})}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} \tag{4-83} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{RPOC}\) = RPOC concentration, g C m⁻³
    • \(K_{LPOC \to RPOC}\) = transfer rate from LPOC to RPOC, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{RPOC}\) = RPOC decay rate, s⁻¹

    LDOC¶

    The kinetic source/sink term for LDOC is:

    \[ S_{LDOC} = \underbrace{\sum K_{am}\delta_{ca}\Phi_a(1-P_{am})}_{\text{algal mortality}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{ae}\delta_{ca}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal excretion}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{em}\delta_{ce}\Phi_e(1-P_{em})}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{ee}\delta_{ce}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton excretion}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LDOC}\Phi_{LDOC}}_{\text{LDOC decay}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LDOC \to RDOC}\Phi_{LDOC}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOC transfer}}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{mm}\delta_{cm}\Phi_{macro}(1-P_{mm})}_{\text{macrophyte mortality}} + \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LPOM \to LDOM}\Phi_{LPOC}}_{\text{LPOC hydrolysis}} + \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{RPOM \to LDOM}\Phi_{RPOC}}_{\text{RPOC hydrolysis}} \tag{4-84} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{LDOC}\) = LDOC concentration, g C m⁻³
    • \(K_{LDOC}\) = LDOC decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{LDOC \to RDOC}\) = LDOC to RDOC transfer rate, s⁻¹

    RDOC¶

    The kinetic source/sink term for RDOC is:

    \[ S_{RDOC} = \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{LDOC \to RDOC}\Phi_{LDOC}}_{\substack{\text{labile to refractory} \\ \text{DOC transfer}}} - \underbrace{\gamma_{om}K_{RDOC}\Phi_{RDOC}}_{\text{RDOC decay}} \tag{4-85} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{RDOC}\) = RDOC concentration, g C m⁻³
    • \(K_{RDOC}\) = RDOC decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{LDOC \to RDOC}\) = LDOC to RDOC transfer rate, s⁻¹

    Carbonaceous Biochemical Oxygen Demand (CBOD)¶

    Any number of CBOD groups with varying decay rates and either particulate or dissolved can be modeled, allowing the user to more accurately characterize various CBOD sources. Additionally, different CBOD sources can be tracked separately in the model to determine their effect on the receiving water.

    When including CBOD in the simulation, ensure that CBOD, DOM, POM, and algal biomass are properly accounted for to avoid double-counting oxygen demand. CBOD is typically specified as allochthonous inputs, while autochthonous organic matter is tracked in the various organic matter compartments. This group can model dissolved or particulate CBOD by specifying a zero or a finite settling velocity, respectively. Settled CBOD enters the first-order sediment compartment.

    In Version 3.6 and earlier, the CBOD cycle used fixed stoichiometry in terms of C:N:P. Starting with Version 3.7, the nitrogen and phosphorus corresponding to each CBOD group are modeled as separate constituents.

    The rate equation for CBOD is:

    \[ S_{CBOD} = -\underbrace{\Theta^{T-20}K_{BOD}R_{BOD}\Phi_{CBOD}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{\omega_{CBOD}\frac{\partial R_{BOD}\Phi_{CBOD}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-86} \]

    where:

    • \(\Theta\) = BOD temperature rate multiplier
    • \(T\) = temperature, °C
    • \(\omega_{CBOD}\) = CBOD settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{BOD}\) = CBOD decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(R_{BOD}\) = CBOD conversion from input CBOD to CBOD-ultimate (CBODᵤ)
    • \(\Phi_{CBOD}\) = CBOD concentration, g m⁻³

    Note

    The model accepts 5-day CBOD₅ or any other day CBOD as input. The \(R_{BOD}\) term converts to CBODᵤ (ultimate or 28-day) internally. The model outputs CBOD in the same form as the input (e.g., CBOD₅ if CBOD₅ was entered). If CBODᵤ is used as input, set \(R_{BOD}\) = 1.

    Typical determination of \(R_{BOD}\) is based on the following standard CBOD equation:

    \[ R_{BOD} = \frac{CBOD_u}{CBOD_t} = \frac{1}{1 - \exp(-K_{BOD} t)} \]

    where \(t\) is the time in days (5 days, for example), \(K_{BOD}\) is the BOD decay rate in day⁻¹, and \(CBOD_t\) is the CBOD at that time.

    CBODP¶

    The phosphorus associated with a specific CBOD group is modeled as a separate constituent. The rate equation for CBODP is:

    \[ S_{CBODP} = -\underbrace{\Theta^{T-20}K_{BOD}\Phi_{CBODP}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{\omega_{CBOD}\frac{\partial \Phi_{CBODP}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \]

    where:

    • \(\Theta\) = BOD temperature rate multiplier
    • \(T\) = temperature, °C
    • \(\omega_{CBOD}\) = CBOD settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{BOD}\) = CBOD decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{CBODP}\) = CBODP concentration, g m⁻³

    CBODN¶

    The nitrogen associated with a specific CBOD group is modeled as a separate constituent. The rate equation for CBODN is:

    \[ S_{CBODN} = -\underbrace{\Theta^{T-20}K_{BOD}\Phi_{CBODN}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{\omega_{CBOD}\frac{\partial \Phi_{CBODN}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \]

    where:

    • \(\Theta\) = BOD temperature rate multiplier
    • \(T\) = temperature, °C
    • \(\omega_{CBOD}\) = CBOD settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{BOD}\) = CBOD decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{CBODN}\) = CBODN concentration, g m⁻³

    Algae¶

    An algal community can be represented as a single assemblage or as multiple algae groups. Often, the algal groups might include diatoms, greens, and cyanobacteria. The user has complete freedom in how many and what kinds of algal groups can be included in the simulation through careful specification of the kinetic rate parameters that define the characteristics of each algal group.

    Source-Sink Term for Algae¶

    The rate equation for each algal group is:

    \[ S_a = \underbrace{K_{ag}\Phi_a}_{\text{growth}} - \underbrace{K_{ar}\Phi_a}_{\text{respiration}} - \underbrace{K_{ae}\Phi_a}_{\text{excretion}} - \underbrace{K_{am}\Phi_a}_{\text{mortality}} - \underbrace{\omega_a\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A\Phi_a}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} - \underbrace{\sum\left(Z_\mu\Phi_{zoo}\frac{\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a}{\sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{lpom} + \sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo}}\right)}_{\text{net loss to grazing}} \tag{4-87} \]

    where:

    • \(z\) = cell height
    • \(A\) = horizontal area
    • \(Z_\mu\) = net growth rate of a zooplankton species
    • \(\sigma_{alg}, \sigma_{zoo}\) = zooplankton grazing preference factors for algae (alg) or zooplankton (zoo)
    • \(K_{ag}\) = algal growth rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ar}\) = algal dark respiration rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ae}\) = algal excretion rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{am}\) = algal mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_a\) = algal settling rate, m s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_a\) = algal concentration, g m⁻³

    Chlorophyll a (chl a) is the most commonly available estimate of algal biomass. To convert chl a to algal biomass, multiply chl a by the ratio of algae (g m⁻³ dry weight organic matter) to chl a (µg L⁻¹).

    Algal Growth Rate¶

    Algal growth rate is computed by modifying a maximum growth rate affected by temperature, light, and nutrient availability:

    \[ K_{ag} = \gamma_{ar}\gamma_{af}\lambda_{\min}K_{ag\max} \tag{4-88} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{ar}\) = temperature rate multiplier for rising limb of curve
    • \(\gamma_{af}\) = temperature rate multiplier for falling limb of curve
    • \(\lambda_{\min}\) = multiplier for limiting growth factor (minimum of light, phosphorus, silica, and nitrogen) between 0 and 1
    • \(K_{ag}\) = algal growth rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ag\max}\) = maximum algal growth rate, s⁻¹

    Light Limitation¶

    The rate multiplier, \(\lambda_l\), between 0 and 1 for light is based upon the Steele (1962) function:

    \[ \lambda_l = F(I) = \frac{I}{I_s}e^{-\frac{I}{I_s}+1} \tag{4-89} \]

    where:

    • \(I\) = available light, W m⁻²
    • \(I_s\) = saturating light intensity at maximum photosynthetic rate, W m⁻²

    The above expression allows for simulation of photoinhibition at light intensities greater than the saturation value. Light penetration decreases with depth:

    \[ I(z) = (1-\beta)I_0 e^{-\alpha z} \tag{4-90} \]

    where:

    • \(I_0\) = solar radiation at the water surface, W m⁻²
    • \(\alpha\) = attenuation coefficient, m⁻¹
    • \(z\) = depth, m
    • \((1-\beta)\) = fraction of solar radiation absorbed at the water surface

    The average effect of light on algal growth in a particular model cell can be obtained by combining the above two expressions and integrating over the cell depth to obtain (Chapra and Reckhow, 1983):

    \[ \lambda_l = \frac{e}{\alpha \Delta z}\left[e^{-\gamma_2} - e^{-\gamma_1}\right] \tag{4-91} \]

    where:

    \[ \gamma_1 = \frac{(1-\beta)I_0 e^{-\alpha d}}{I_s} \]
    \[ \gamma_2 = \frac{(1-\beta)I_0 e^{-\alpha(d+\Delta z)}}{I_s} \]

    and \(d\) is the depth at the top of the model cell, m.

    The fraction of solar radiation, \((1-\beta)I_0\), is added directly to the surface layer. The attenuation coefficient, \(\alpha\), consists of a baseline value [EXH2O] to which the effects of inorganic [EXINOR] and organic [EXORG] suspended solids, and algae [EXA] are added.

    Nutrient Limitation¶

    Rate multipliers limiting maximum algal growth due to nutrient limitations are computed using the Monod relationship:

    \[ \lambda_i = \frac{\Phi_i}{P_i + \Phi_i} \tag{4-92} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_i\) = phosphorus or nitrate + ammonium concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(P_i\) = half-saturation coefficient for phosphorus or nitrate + ammonium, g m⁻³

    Ammonium Preference¶

    The algal nitrogen preference for ammonium is based upon the following (Thomann and Fitzpatrick, 1982) equation:

    \[ P_{NH4} = \Phi_{NH4}\frac{\Phi_{NOx}}{(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NH4})(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})} + \Phi_{NH4}\frac{K_{NH4}}{(\Phi_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})} \tag{4-93} \]

    where:

    • \(P_{NH4}\) = ammonium preference factor
    • \(K_{NH4}\) = ammonia preference half-saturation coefficient, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{NH4}\) = ammonium concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{NOx}\) = nitrate-nitrite concentration, g m⁻³

    This allows algae to use primarily ammonium and gradually switch to nitrate as ammonium concentrations decrease.

    Algal Dark Respiration¶

    Algal dark respiration is computed using the rising limb of the temperature function:

    \[ K_{ar} = \gamma_{ar}\gamma_{af}K_{ar\max} \tag{4-94} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{ar}\) = temperature rate multiplier for rising limb of curve
    • \(\gamma_{af}\) = temperature rate multiplier for falling limb of curve
    • \(K_{ar\max}\) = maximum dark respiration rate, s⁻¹

    Algal Excretion¶

    Algal photorespiration (excretion) is evaluated using an inverse relation to the light rate multiplier:

    \[ K_{ae} = (1-\lambda_l)\gamma_{ar}\gamma_{af}K_{ae\max} \tag{4-95} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{ar}\) = temperature rate multiplier for rising limb of curve
    • \(\gamma_{af}\) = temperature rate multiplier for falling limb of curve
    • \(K_{ae\max}\) = maximum excretion rate constant, s⁻¹
    • \(\lambda_l\) = light limiting factor

    Excretion rates increase at both low and high light intensities, with excretion products contributing to labile DOM.

    Algal Mortality¶

    Algal mortality is defined as:

    \[ K_{am} = \gamma_{ar}\gamma_{af}K_{am\max} \tag{4-96} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{ar}\) = temperature rate multiplier for rising limb of curve
    • \(\gamma_{af}\) = temperature rate multiplier for falling limb of curve
    • \(K_{am\max}\) = maximum mortality rate, s⁻¹

    This mortality rate represents both natural and predator mortality. Algal growth does not occur in the absence of light. Algal growth is not allowed to exceed the limit imposed by nutrient supply over a given timestep. Algal excretion is not allowed to exceed algal growth rates.

    Like inorganic solids, settling algae serve as a source for the layer below. Unlike inorganic solids, algae passing to the sediments accumulate within the sediment compartment. POM is also accumulated in this sediment compartment.

    Algae Vertical Migration¶

    Some species of cyanobacteria, often responsible for harmful algal blooms (HABs), can move vertically in the water column through their own motility, independent of water velocity. This vertical migration can lead to HABs when colonies accumulate at the water surface and experience increased growth, degrading water quality and environmental health.

    Cyanobacteria vertical migration is achieved through a process called buoyancy regulation. Cells regulate their buoyancy either with carbohydrate ballast or gas vesicles. Two different models were used to calculate a settling velocity for an algae group at each time step.

    Predefined Velocity¶

    A simple way to model the vertical movement of cyanobacteria is to assume a velocity function for colonies based on knowledge of their typical movement. An equation for colony velocity as a function of time can be used:

    \[ v_p(t) = A\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}t + \phi\right) \]

    where \(A\) is the migration amplitude and the period is assumed to be one day (86,400 seconds). The value of the phase (\(\phi\)) depends on the initial location of colonies.

    The velocity function can also be dependent on space as well as time:

    \[ v_p(t,z) = \begin{cases} A\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}t + \phi\right)e^{-\alpha(H-z)}, & I_0 > 0 \\ A\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{86{,}400\text{ s}}t + \phi\right), & I_0 \leq 0 \end{cases} \]

    where \(\alpha\) is the light attenuation coefficient and \(I_0\) is solar irradiance at the water surface, \(H\) is a user-supplied depth parameter. The exponential term is only applied during the photoperiod.

    Dynamic Velocity¶

    The predefined velocity approach can predict cyanobacteria movement based on the observed tendency of colonies to migrate vertically on a daily cycle; however, it does not reflect the response of colonies to variations in solar irradiance. The dynamic velocity model uses colony density computed based on solar irradiance:

    \[ \frac{\partial \rho_c}{\partial t} = c_1 F(I) - c_2 \]

    where \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are calibration coefficients. In the absence of light, density decreases at a constant rate. The numerical solution is:

    \[ \rho_{c_i}^{n+1} = (c_1 F(I_i^n) - c_2)\Delta t + \rho_{c_i}^n \]

    The settling velocity is then solved via Stokes' law:

    \[ v = \frac{2gr^2(\rho_c - \rho')}{9\phi n} \]

    where \(g\) is acceleration due to gravity, \(r\) is colony radius, \(\rho_c\) is cyanobacteria density, \(\rho'\) is density of water, \(\phi\) is form resistance, and \(n\) is viscosity of water.

    An exponentially-decaying weighted average of past growth rates in each grid cell was applied:

    \[ \rho_{c_i}^{n+1} = \frac{\sum_{q=-1}^{Q} \rho_{c_i}^{n-q} W^q}{\sum_{q=-1}^{Q} W^q} \]

    where:

    \[ W^q = e^{-k(t^{n+1}-t^{n-q})} \]

    and \(k\) is the time decay constant for influence of past densities.

    Periphyton/Epiphyton¶

    Any number of user-defined periphyton/epiphyton groups can be modeled. Similar to the first-order sediment compartment, periphyton/epiphyton are not transported in the water column.

    The rate equation for each epiphyton/periphyton group is:

    \[ S_e = \underbrace{K_{eg}\Phi_e}_{\text{growth}} - \underbrace{K_{er}\Phi_e}_{\text{respiration}} - \underbrace{K_{ee}\Phi_e}_{\text{excretion}} - \underbrace{K_{em}\Phi_e}_{\text{mortality}} - \underbrace{K_{eb}\Phi_e}_{\text{burial}} \tag{4-99} \]

    where:

    • \(K_{eg}\) = epiphyton growth rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{er}\) = epiphyton dark respiration rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ee}\) = epiphyton excretion rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{em}\) = epiphyton mortality rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{eb}\) = epiphyton burial rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_e\) = epiphyton concentration, g m⁻³

    Epiphyton Growth Rate¶

    Epiphyton/periphyton growth rate is computed by modifying a maximum growth rate affected by epiphyton/periphyton biomass, temperature, and nutrient availability:

    \[ K_{eg} = \gamma_{er}\gamma_{ef}\lambda_{\min}K_{eg\max} \tag{4-100} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{er}\) = temperature rate multiplier for rising limb of curve
    • \(\gamma_{ef}\) = temperature rate multiplier for falling limb of curve
    • \(\lambda_{\min}\) = multiplier for limiting growth factor (minimum of phosphorus, silica, nitrogen, and epiphyton/periphyton biomass) between 0 and 1
    • \(K_{eg\max}\) = maximum epiphyton/periphyton growth rate, s⁻¹

    The rate multiplier for light is based upon the Steele (1962) function:

    \[ \lambda_l = F(I) = \frac{I}{I_s}e^{-\frac{I}{I_s}+1} \tag{4-101} \]

    Light penetration decreases with depth:

    \[ I(z) = (1-\beta)I_0 e^{-\alpha z} \tag{4-102} \]

    The average effect of light on epiphyton/periphyton growth in a model cell is:

    \[ \lambda_l = \frac{e}{\alpha \Delta z}\left[e^{-\gamma_2} - e^{-\gamma_1}\right] \tag{4-103} \]

    The attenuation coefficient, \(\alpha\), is computed from a baseline value [EXH2O] to which the effects of inorganic [EXINOR] and organic [EXORG] suspended solids, as well as the extinction of each algal group, are added. Epiphyton/periphyton self-shading is accounted for in the biomass limitation formulation.

    Nutrient limitation uses the Monod relationship:

    \[ \lambda_i = \frac{\Phi_i}{P_i + \Phi_i} \tag{4-104} \]

    The epiphyton/periphyton preference for ammonium is modeled using (Thomann and Fitzpatrick, 1982):

    \[ P_{NH4} = \Phi_{NH4}\frac{\Phi_{NOx}}{(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NH4})(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})} + \Phi_{NH4}\frac{K_{NH4}}{(\Phi_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})(K_{NH4}+\Phi_{NOx})} \tag{4-105} \]

    Epiphyton Respiration, Excretion, Mortality¶

    Epiphyton/periphyton dark respiration:

    \[ K_{er} = \gamma_{er}\gamma_{ef}K_{er\max} \tag{4-106} \]

    Epiphyton/periphyton excretion:

    \[ K_{ee} = (1-\lambda_l)\gamma_{er}\gamma_{ef}K_{ee\max} \tag{4-107} \]

    Epiphyton/periphyton mortality:

    \[ K_{em} = \gamma_{er}\gamma_{ef}K_{em\max} \tag{4-108} \]

    Epiphyton/periphyton that die (mortality) become a part of the labile particulate organic matter and the labile dissolved organic matter pool. The user defines the fraction of the dead epiphyton (EPOM) that goes to the LPOM pool. The epiphyton/periphyton burial rate represents the burial of dead epiphyton/periphyton to the organic sediment compartment and becomes part of the first-order sediment compartment.

    Biomass Limitation¶

    The epiphyton/periphyton biomass is controlled by a biomass limitation equation based on Monod kinetics. The biomass limitation function, \(f\) [BLIM], varies from 0 to 1 and is multiplied with the growth rate:

    \[ f = \left[1 - \frac{B}{B + K_B}\right] \tag{4-109} \]

    where:

    • \(B\) = epiphyton areal biomass, g m⁻² [EPD]
    • \(K_B\) = epiphyton areal biomass half-saturation coefficient, g m⁻² [EHS]

    The areal biomass is calculated as:

    \[ B = \Phi_e \frac{V}{A} \tag{4-110} \]

    where:

    • \(A\) = computational cell surface area including side walls and bottom area, m²
    • \(V\) = computational cell volume, m³
    • \(\Phi_e\) = epiphyton concentration, g m⁻³

    The biomass limitation is a surrogate calibration parameter for light limitation due to self-shading.

    Macrophytes¶

    The macrophyte model consists of two parts: a section describing the water quality compartment and a section describing the hydrodynamic compartment. Both sections are from Berger (2000) and Berger and Wells (2008).

    The macrophyte model simulates multiple submerged macrophyte species without differentiating between plant parts. Light, temperature, carbon dioxide, ammonia-nitrogen (the sole nitrogen source for macrophytes), and ortho-phosphorus may limit growth. Depending on the macrophyte species, nitrogen and phosphorus may be obtained from the sediments or the water column. Plants grow upward from the sediment through model layers. Macrophyte shading is modeled by making light attenuation a function of macrophyte concentration.

    The macrophyte growth rate is modeled as:

    \[ S_{macro} = \underbrace{\mu_{m\max}f(I,N,P,C)\gamma_1\gamma_2\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{growth}} - \underbrace{K_{mr}\gamma_1\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{respiration}} - \underbrace{K_{mm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{mortality}} \tag{4-111} \]

    where:

    • \(S_{macro}\) = macrophyte growth rate density, g m⁻³ s⁻¹
    • \(I\) = solar radiation, W m⁻²
    • \(f(I,N,P,C)\) = growth limiting function between 0 and 1
    • \(\mu_{m\max}\) = maximum macrophyte growth rate (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(K_{mr}\) = maximum respiration rate (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(K_{mm}\) = mortality/excretion rate (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(\gamma_1\) = ascending temperature rate multiplier
    • \(\gamma_2\) = descending temperature rate multiplier
    • \(\Phi_{macro}\) = macrophyte concentration, g m⁻³

    Growth rate, respiration rate, and mortality/excretion rate are temperature dependent. Temperature effects are modeled using the equations developed by Thornton and Lessem (1978), also used in the phytoplankton compartment of CE-QUAL-W2. The growth limiting function \(f(I,N,P,C)\) is the minimum of the light \(f(I)\), nitrogen \(f(N)\), phosphorus \(f(P)\), and carbon \(f(C)\) limiting functions:

    \[ f(I,N,P,C) = \min(f(I), f(N), f(P), f(C)) \]

    All the limiting functions are unitless and have a value between 0 and 1. The limiting functions for the nutrients have the Michaelis-Menten form:

    \[ f(S) = \frac{S}{K_S + S} \tag{4-112} \]

    where \(S\) is the nutrient concentration (g m⁻³) and \(K_S\) is the half-saturation concentration (g m⁻³).

    Light limitation is modeled with a hyperbolic equation:

    \[ f(I) = \frac{I}{I + I_h} \tag{4-113} \]

    where \(I\) is solar radiation (W m⁻²) and \(I_h\) is the half-saturation coefficient for solar radiation (W m⁻²). This function is frequently used in the absence of photoinhibition.

    The net light extinction coefficient (m⁻¹) is modeled as a function of macrophyte plant tissue concentration:

    \[ \gamma = \varepsilon_{H_2O} + \varepsilon_{iss}\Phi_{iss} + \varepsilon_{oss}\Phi_{oss} + \sum_{\substack{\text{macrophyte} \\ \text{groups}}} \varepsilon_{mac}\Phi_{mac} + \sum_{\substack{\text{algae} \\ \text{groups}}} \varepsilon_{algae}\Phi_{algae} \tag{4-114} \]

    where:

    • \(\varepsilon_{H_2O}\) = light extinction exclusive of suspended solids, m⁻¹ [EXH2O]
    • \(\varepsilon_{iss}\) = light extinction due to inorganic suspended solids, m² g⁻¹ [EXINOR]
    • \(\varepsilon_{oss}\) = light extinction due to non-living organic suspended solids, m² g⁻¹ [EXORG]
    • \(\varepsilon_{mac}\) = light extinction due to macrophytes, m² g⁻¹ [EXM]
    • \(\varepsilon_{algae}\) = light extinction due to algae, m² g⁻¹ [EXA]

    Modeling Frictional Force¶

    Modeling of flow through macrophytes incorporates the following concepts:

    • Porosity of the macrophytes was calculated through determination of the blockage area of the vegetation normal to the direction of flow
    • The drag of individual stems and leaves were totaled to determine the total drag force in a model cell
    • The effective Manning's \(n\) was calculated by combining the effect of bed shear and the drag force on the plants

    The total frictional force \(f\) was partitioned into a bottom friction component \(f_b\) and a vegetation drag component \(f_v\):

    \[ f = f_b + f_v \tag{4-115} \]

    Bottom shear \(\tau_b\) was simulated using Manning's friction factor:

    \[ \tau_b = \frac{\rho_w g n^2}{R^{1/3}} U|U| \tag{4-116} \]

    The effective Manning's \(n\) of each model cell was calculated in the manner used by Petryk and Bosmajian (1975):

    \[ n = n_b\sqrt{1 + \frac{C_d \sum A_i}{2gAL}\frac{1}{n_b^2}R^{4/3}} \tag{4-117} \]

    where \(n_b\) is the Manning's friction factor due to bed shear only.

    Modeling Porosity¶

    The volume \(V_m\) of macrophytes can be estimated by dividing the macrophyte mass in a model cell \(m\) by density \(\rho_m\):

    \[ V_m = \frac{m}{\rho_m} \]

    The porosity \(\varphi\) was estimated by dividing volume within a cell free of macrophytes by the cell total volume \(V\):

    \[ \varphi = \left(\frac{V - V_m}{V}\right) \]

    Changes to Governing Equations¶

    Several of the governing equations have been altered to account for porosity and the frictional effects of macrophytes. The new x-momentum equation is:

    \[ \frac{\partial U\varphi B}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial UU\varphi B}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial WU\varphi B}{\partial z} = -\frac{\varphi B}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial \varphi B\tau_{xx}}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial \varphi B\tau_{xz}}{\partial z} \tag{4-118} \]

    The continuity equation was changed to:

    \[ \frac{\partial U\varphi B}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial W\varphi B}{\partial z} = q\varphi B \tag{4-121} \]

    The new free water surface equation is:

    \[ \frac{\partial B_\eta \varphi \eta}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int_\eta^h U\varphi B dz - \int_\eta^h q\varphi B dz \tag{4-122} \]

    The constituent transport equation was modified to:

    \[ \frac{\partial \varphi BC}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial U\varphi BC}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial W\varphi BC}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\varphi BD_x\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\varphi BD_z\frac{\partial C}{\partial z} = q_\varphi\varphi B + S_K\varphi B \tag{4-123} \]

    Zooplankton¶

    The zooplankton compartment was adapted from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) reservoir model CE-QUAL-R1 (Environmental Laboratory, 1995). Zooplankton are assumed to be non-motile and are transported only by advection and dispersion. Zooplankton can graze algae, detritus (POM), and other zooplankton. Losses occur through mortality and respiration. Algae vertical settling or floating can also be specified through a settling velocity and dynamic vertical migration.

    The source/sink term for zooplankton is:

    \[ S_{zoo} = \underbrace{\gamma_1\gamma_2 Z_e K_{z\max}\left(\frac{(\sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{lpom} + \sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo}) - Z_L}{(\sum\sigma_{alg}\Phi_a + \sigma_{pom}\Phi_{lpom} + \sum\sigma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo}) + Z_{1/2}}\right)\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{growth}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{(1-\gamma_2)K_{zm}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{mortality}} - \underbrace{\gamma_1 K_{zr}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{respiration}} - \underbrace{\omega_{zoo}\frac{\partial \Phi_{zoo}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{lpom}\) = labile particulate organic matter concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_a\) = algae concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{zoo}\) = zooplankton concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(K_{zm}\) = zooplankton mortality rate (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(K_{z\max}\) = maximum ingestion rate for zooplankton (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(K_{zr}\) = zooplankton respiration rate (input: d⁻¹; internal: s⁻¹)
    • \(Z_{1/2}\) = half-saturation coefficient for zooplankton ingestion, g m⁻³
    • \(Z_e\) = zooplankton ingestion efficiency
    • \(Z_L\) = low threshold concentration for zooplankton feeding, g m⁻³
    • \(\gamma_1\) = temperature coefficient for rising limb of curve for zooplankton
    • \(\gamma_2\) = temperature coefficient for falling limb of curve for zooplankton
    • \(\sigma_{alg}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for algae
    • \(\sigma_{zoo}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for zooplankton
    • \(\sigma_{pom}\) = zooplankton preference fraction for particulate organic matter
    • \(\omega_{zoo}\) = zooplankton settling velocity

    The growth rate is a function of temperature, the maximum growth rate, and a modified Michaelis-Menten equation that includes a low threshold concentration below which zooplankton do not feed. At dissolved oxygen concentrations below 2 g m⁻³, feeding stops and the mortality rate is doubled.

    Phosphorus¶

    Phosphorus is an important element in aquatic ecosystems since it serves as one of the primary nutrients for phytoplankton growth. In many fresh waters, phosphorus is considered to be the nutrient limiting maximum production of phytoplankton biomass.

    Phosphorus is assumed to be completely available as ortho-phosphate (PO₄) for uptake by phytoplankton. Measurements of soluble reactive phosphorus are closest to the form used in the model. Macrophytes are specified as either taking P from the sediments or from the water column.

    The rate equation for phosphorus is:

    \[ S_P = \underbrace{\sum(K_{ar}-K_{ag})\delta_{Pa}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal net growth}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{er}-K_{eg})\delta_{Pe}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton net growth}} + \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\delta_{POM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\text{labile DOM decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{RDOM}\delta_{POM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RDOM}}_{\text{refractory DOM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\delta_{POM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\text{labile POM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{RPOM}\delta_{POM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RPOM}}_{\text{refractory POM decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{CBOD}R_{CBOD}\delta_{P-CBOD}\Theta^{T-20}\Phi_{CBOD}}_{\text{CBOD decay}} + \underbrace{K_s\delta_{POM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_s}_{\text{1st-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{SOD\gamma_{OM}\frac{A_{sed}}{V}}_{\text{0-order sediment release}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\frac{(\sum\omega_{ISS}\Phi_{ISS}+\omega_{Fe}\Phi_{Fe})P_P}{\Delta z}\Phi_P}_{\text{inorganic solids adsorption}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{mr}-(1-f_{psed})K_{mg})\delta_{Pm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{macrophyte net growth}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zr}\delta_{Pz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton respiration}} \tag{4-124} \]

    where:

    • \(\Delta z\) = model cell thickness, m
    • \(A_{sed}\) = sediment surface area, m²
    • \(V\) = cell volume, m³
    • \(P_P\) = adsorption coefficient, m³ g⁻¹
    • \(f_{psed}\) = fraction of macrophyte phosphorus uptake from sediments
    • \(\delta_{Pe}\) = periphyton/epiphyton stoichiometric coefficient for phosphorus
    • \(\delta_{Pa}\) = algal stoichiometric coefficient for phosphorus
    • \(\delta_{Pm}\) = macrophyte stoichiometric coefficient for phosphorus
    • \(\delta_{Pz}\) = zooplankton stoichiometric coefficient for phosphorus
    • \(\delta_{POM}\) = organic matter stoichiometric coefficient for phosphorus
    • \(\delta_{P-CBOD}\) = phosphorus/CBOD stoichiometric ratio
    • \(SOD\) = sediment oxygen demand, g m⁻² s⁻¹
    • \(K_s\) = sediment decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_P\) = phosphorus concentration, g m⁻³

    Dissolved inorganic phosphorus adsorbs onto inorganic particulates under oxic conditions and is lost when these materials settle. A Langmuir isotherm describes this process. Since phosphorus concentrations are generally small, only the isotherm's linear region is utilized and is represented by the product \(P_P \Phi_P\). Adsorption is not allowed to occur if dissolved oxygen concentrations approach anaerobic conditions.

    Warning

    Phosphorus adsorption onto inorganic suspended solids should be used cautiously. In most systems, available phosphorus sites for adsorption onto inflowing inorganic suspended solids are generally already in use.

    Sediment contribution of phosphorus to overlying waters can be simulated in three ways:

    1. The sediment compartment accumulates particulate organic matter and algae, which then decay under oxic conditions. This is modeled as a first-order process.
    2. Under anaerobic conditions using the zero-order sediment oxygen model, sediments can be assigned a release rate for phosphorus that is a function of the assigned sediment oxygen demand rate.
    3. Sediment diagenesis predicts P release under anaerobic conditions. This is a truly predictive model based on organic matter and Fe-adsorbed P loading to the sediments.

    Ammonium¶

    Ammonium is a product of the decay of organic matter, and algae can use ammonium during photosynthesis to form proteins. In many estuarine applications, nitrogen is the limiting nutrient for algal growth. Macrophytes can prefer taking N from the sediments versus the water column. Ammonia can decay in the presence of nitrifying bacteria to nitrite and nitrate. Ammonium is viewed as total ammonia, both ammonium (NH₄⁺-N) and unionized ammonia (NH₃-N).

    The rate equation for ammonium is:

    \[ S_{NH4} = \underbrace{\sum K_{ar}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal respiration}} - \underbrace{\sum K_{ag}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a P_{NH4}}_{\text{algal growth}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{er}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton respiration}} - \underbrace{\sum K_{eg}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e P_{NH4}}_{\text{epiphyton growth}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\delta_{NOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\text{labile DOM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{RDOM}\delta_{NOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RDOM}}_{\text{refractory DOM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\delta_{NOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\text{labile POM decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{RPOM}\delta_{NOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RPOM}}_{\text{refractory POM decay}} + \underbrace{K_s\delta_{NOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_s}_{\text{1st-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{SOD_{NH4}\gamma_{OM}\frac{A_{sed}}{V}}_{\text{0-order sediment release}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{CBOD}R_{CBOD}\delta_{N-CBOD}\Theta^{T-20}\Phi_{CBOD}}_{\text{CBOD decay}} - \underbrace{K_{NH4}\gamma_{NH4}\Phi_{NH4}}_{\text{nitrification}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum(K_{mr}-(1-f_{nsed})K_{mg})\delta_{Nm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{net macrophyte growth}} + \underbrace{\sum K_{zr}\delta_{Nz}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton respiration}} \tag{4-125} \]

    The rate terms are evaluated in subroutine AMMONIUM. As with phosphorus, zero-order sediment release only occurs when dissolved oxygen is less than a minimum value as determined by the transition from aerobic to anaerobic processes. Either a zero-order or first-order process or a combination of both may be used for sediment ammonium release. The sediment diagenesis model also computes ammonium release during anaerobic conditions. In the model, the ammonium concentration is in units of NH₄ as N.

    Unionized ammonia is also a derived constituent in CE-QUAL-W2 and is derived from temperature, NH₄-Total, and pH.

    Nitrate-Nitrite¶

    This compartment represents nitrate plus nitrite. Nitrite is an intermediate product in nitrification between ammonium and nitrate. Nitrate can be used as a source of nitrogen for algae and periphyton/epiphyton during photosynthesis based on the ammonia-nitrate preference factor set for each algal and periphyton group.

    Nitrogen may be the limiting nutrient for algae in systems with high phosphorus loadings or in estuaries. Some species of cyanobacteria can fix atmospheric nitrogen for use in photosynthesis. This process can be included by setting the nitrogen half-saturation concentration for algal growth to zero.

    The rate equation for nitrate-nitrite is:

    \[ S_{NOx} = \underbrace{K_{NH4}\gamma_{NH4}\Phi_{NH4}}_{\text{nitrification}} - \underbrace{K_{NOx}\gamma_{NOx}\Phi_{NOx}}_{\text{water column denitrification}} - \underbrace{\omega_{NOx}\frac{\partial \Phi_{NOx}}{\partial z}}_{\text{sediment denitrification}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum K_{ag}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a(1-P_{NH4})}_{\text{algal uptake}} - \underbrace{\sum K_{eg}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e(1-P_{NH4})}_{\text{epiphyton uptake}} \tag{4-126} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{NH4}\) = temperature rate multiplier for nitrification
    • \(\gamma_{NOx}\) = temperature rate multiplier for denitrification
    • \(\delta_{Ne}\) = epiphyton stoichiometric coefficient for nitrogen
    • \(\delta_{Na}\) = algal stoichiometric coefficient for nitrogen
    • \(P_{NH4}\) = ammonium preference factor
    • \(K_{NH4}\) = nitrification rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{NOx}\) = denitrification rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{ag}\) = algal growth rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_{NOx}\) = sediment transfer velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_{NH4}\) = ammonia-nitrogen concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{NOx}\) = nitrate-nitrogen concentration, g m⁻³

    Nitrification is only allowed to occur if oxygen is present and is gradually decreased as oxygen is depleted. Denitrification is allowed to occur as oxygen is depleted based on the transition from aerobic to anaerobic processes (following a Monod approach). In the model, the nitrate+nitrite concentration is in units of NO₃ + NO₂ as N.

    Dissolved Silica¶

    Dissolved silica is an important component of diatoms, providing the structural skeleton. In many cases diatoms can be silica limited. Dissolved silica is taken up by algae based on stoichiometric relationships and is produced by the decay of organic matter containing particulate biogenic silica. Dissolved silica is also adsorbed onto inorganic suspended solids based on a partitioning coefficient.

    The rate equation for dissolved silica is:

    \[ S_{DSi} = -\underbrace{\sum(K_{ag}-K_{ar})\delta_{Sia}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal uptake}} - \underbrace{\sum(K_{eg}-K_{er})\delta_{Sie}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton uptake}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{sed}\delta_{SiOM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{sed}}_{\text{1st-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{SOD\varphi_{Si}\gamma_{OM}\frac{A_{sed}}{V}}_{\text{0-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{K_{PSi}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{PSi}}_{\substack{\text{particulate biogenic} \\ \text{silica decay}}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\frac{P_{Si}(\sum\omega_{SS}\Phi_{SS} + \omega_{POM}\Phi_{POML} + \omega_{POM}\Phi_{POMR} + \omega_{Fe}\Phi_{Fe})}{\Delta z}\Phi_{DSi}}_{\text{inorganic solids adsorption and settling}} \tag{4-127} \]

    Particulate Biogenic Silica¶

    Particulate biogenic silica results from diatom mortality and settles and also dissolves to form dissolved silica.

    The rate equation for particulate biogenic silica is:

    \[ S_{PSi} = \underbrace{P_{am}\delta_{Sia}K_{am}\Phi_a}_{\text{algae mortality}} + \underbrace{P_{em}\delta_{Sie}K_{em}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton mortality}} - \underbrace{K_{PSi}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{PSi}}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{\frac{\omega_{PSi}\partial \Phi_{PSi}}{\partial z}}_{\text{settling}} \tag{4-128} \]

    where:

    • \(P_{am}\) = partition coefficient for algal mortality
    • \(\delta_{Sie}\) = epiphyton stoichiometric coefficient for silica
    • \(\delta_{Sia}\) = algal stoichiometric coefficient for silica
    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = temperature rate multiplier for organic matter
    • \(K_{PSi}\) = particulate biogenic silica decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_{PSi}\) = particulate biogenic silica settling rate, m s⁻¹

    Dissolved Oxygen¶

    Oxygen is one of the most important elements in aquatic ecosystems. It is essential for higher forms of life, controls many chemical reactions through oxidation, and is a surrogate variable indicating the general health of aquatic systems.

    CE-QUAL-W2 includes both aerobic and anaerobic processes. The ability to model anaerobic periods is important since it provides information on potential problems with water quality. Simulations can be used to identify possibilities for both metalimnetic and hypolimnetic oxygen depletion and its impact on various water control management alternatives.

    The rate equation for dissolved oxygen is:

    \[ S_{DO} = \underbrace{\sum(K_{ag}-K_{ar})\delta_{OMa}\Phi_a}_{\text{algal net production}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{eg}-K_{er})\delta_{OMe}\Phi_e}_{\text{epiphyton net production}} + \underbrace{\frac{A_{sur}}{V_{sur}}K_L(\Phi_{S_{DO}} - \Phi_{DO})}_{\text{aeration}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{K_{RPOM}\delta_{OM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RPOM}}_{\text{refractory POM decay}} - \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\delta_{OM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\text{labile POM decay}} - \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\gamma_{OM}\delta_{OM}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\text{labile DOM decay}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{K_{RDOM}\delta_{OM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RDOM}}_{\text{refractory DOM decay}} - \underbrace{K_s\delta_{OM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{sed}}_{\text{1st-order sediment decay}} - \underbrace{SOD\gamma_{OM}\frac{A_{sed}}{V}}_{\text{0-order SOD}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum K_{CBOD}R_{CBOD}\Theta^{T-20}\Phi_{CBOD}}_{\text{CBOD decay}} - \underbrace{K_{NH4}\delta_{NH4}\gamma_{NH4}\Phi_{NH4}}_{\text{nitrification}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{mg}-K_{mr})\delta_{OMmac}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{macrophyte net production}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\sum\gamma_{zoo}K_{zr}\delta_{OMzoo}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton respiration}} - \underbrace{\delta_{H2S-O2}K_{H2S}\Phi_{H2S}}_{\text{oxidation}} - \underbrace{\delta_{CH4-O2}K_{CH4}\Phi_{CH4}}_{\text{oxidation}} - \underbrace{\delta_{Fe2-O2}K_{Fe2}\Phi_{Fe2}}_{\text{oxidation}} \]
    \[ - \underbrace{\delta_{Mn2-O2}K_{Mn2}\Phi_{Mn2}}_{\text{oxidation}} \tag{4-129} \]

    The rate terms are evaluated in subroutine DISSOLVED_OXYGEN. Decay is not allowed to occur when dissolved oxygen concentrations are zero. A Monod formulation is used to move gradually from oxic to anoxic conditions:

    \[ \text{Rate Reduction} = \frac{\Phi_{DO}}{K_{DO} + \Phi_{DO}} \]

    where \(\Phi_{DO}\) is the concentration of dissolved oxygen and \(K_{DO}\) is a half-saturation dissolved oxygen concentration when oxic reactions are half of their maximum without limitation of oxygen conditions.

    River Reaeration Equations¶

    Since reaeration in waterbodies is dependent on surface layer turbulence, the reaeration formulae for riverine, lake/reservoir, and estuarine systems are different. Surface layer turbulence for riverine waterbodies is dependent on boundary shear, while for lake or reservoir waterbodies, wind stress is usually the primary component of surface layer turbulence.

    Type of water body \(k_a\) (day⁻¹) at 20 °C
    Small ponds and backwaters 0.10--0.23 day⁻¹
    Sluggish streams and large lakes 0.23--0.35 day⁻¹
    Large streams of low velocity 0.35--0.46 day⁻¹
    Large streams of normal velocity 0.46--0.69 day⁻¹
    Swift streams 0.69--1.15 day⁻¹
    Rapids and waterfalls >1.15 day⁻¹

    The model provides multiple formulations for river reaeration including O'Connor and Dobbins (1958), Churchill, Elmore, and Buckingham (1962), Tsivoglou and Wallace (1972), Owens et al. (1964), Thackston and Krenkel (1966), Langbien and Durum (1967), Melching and Flores (1999), and a user-defined relationship.

    Lake Reaeration Equations¶

    Wind effects rather than boundary shear more often control reaeration in lakes, reservoirs, and estuarine systems. The model provides numerous formulations as a function of wind speed at 10 m, including Broecker et al. (1978), Gelda et al. (1996), Banks and Herrera (1977), Wanninkhof et al. (1991), Cole and Buchak (1995), Banks (1975), Smith (1978), Liss (1973), Downing and Truesdale (1955), Kanwisher (1963), Yu et al. (1977), Weiler (1974), and a user-defined relationship.

    The wind speed at 10 m elevation in the middle of a lake or reservoir, \(W_{10m}\), can be computed from that measured at 10 m on land by using an approach from Fang and Stefan (1994):

    \[ W_{10m} = W_z f(fetch) \tag{4-131} \]
    \[ f(fetch) = \frac{\ln\frac{10}{z_{o2}}\ln\frac{\delta}{z_{o1}}}{\ln\frac{10}{z_{o1}}\ln\frac{\delta}{z_{o2}}} \approx \frac{5ZB + 4.6052}{3ZB + 9.2103} \tag{4-132} \]

    where:

    • \(z_{o1}\) = roughness of land (assume 0.01 m)
    • \(z_{o2}\) = roughness of water surface (assume 0.0001 m)
    • \(\delta\) = thickness of wind boundary layer over smooth surface, m
    • \(ZB = 0.8 \ln\frac{fetch}{2} - 1.0718\)

    Estuarine Equations¶

    For estuarine systems, Thomann and Mueller (1987) and Chapra (1997) suggest using any of the wind formulations or Equation 1 in the river reaeration table using the mean tidal velocity over a tidal cycle for the horizontal velocity. An additional formulation from Thomann and Fitzpatrick (1982) combines the effect of wind and tidal flow.

    For cases where the time scale of tidal flow is much greater than the time scale for reaeration, the reaeration process can be described as a steady process and the impacts of stream flow and wind can be additive:

    \[ K_L = K_{Lb} + K_{Lw} \]

    Reaeration Temperature Dependence¶

    Reaeration temperature dependence is usually based upon an Arrhenius formulation:

    \[ K_T = K_{20}\Theta^{T-20} \tag{4-133} \]

    where \(\Theta\) is 1.024. The molecular diffusivity of oxygen varies according to temperature:

    \[ D_{O2} = 4.58 \times 10^{-11} T + 1.2 \times 10^{-9} \tag{4-134} \]

    where \(D_{O2}\) is molecular diffusivity of oxygen (m² s⁻¹) and \(T\) is temperature (°C).

    Dam Reaeration¶

    Many rivers and reservoirs have spillways or weirs over which water entrains oxygen into a pool below the dam. The model includes approaches for both small dams or weirs and large dam spillways/gates.

    Small Dams or Weirs¶

    Formulae for predicting the entrainment of dissolved oxygen based on empirical data include Barrett et al. (1960), Mastropietro (1968), and Butts and Evans (1983).

    Large Dam Spillways/Gates¶

    The USACE has conducted gas abatement studies on the Columbia and Snake River system for many years. Empirical models for gas production from spillways were developed in CRiSP 1.6. The gas production equations are empirical correlations between TDG, measured approximately one mile downstream of the dam, and discharge.

    Equation type Equation Description
    Linear function of total spill \(\%TDG = mQ_s + b\) \(Q_s\) = total spill, kcfs; \(m\) = empirical coefficient; \(b\) = empirical coefficient
    Bounded exponential of total spill \(\%TDG = a + be^{cQ_s}\) \(a, b, c\) = empirical coefficients
    Bounded exponential per spillway \(\%TDG = a + be^{cq_s}\) \(q_s\) = spill through individual spillway, kcfs

    DO Impacts of Spillways/Weirs or Gates¶

    For each spillway, weir, or gate, the user has the choice of equation to use for computing the effects of hydraulic structures on downstream dissolved oxygen. The model includes options for linear TDG function per spillway, exponential TDG function, small dam reaeration effect, and turbine aeration fractions.

    Note

    For equations 1 and 2 in the spillway reaeration table, the maximum TDG allowed is 145%. If TDG is computed to be less than 100%, there is no effect of the spillway or gate on reaeration.

    SYSTDG Algorithm¶

    The SYSTDG algorithm for computing total dissolved gas (TDG) from spillways was developed by Schneider and Hamilton (2015a, 2015b) to compute real-time TDG saturation levels in the Columbia River basin, USA. SYSTDG is an empirical regression equation for each spillway at a hydropower facility based on flow rate, tailwater depth, and barometric pressure. Zhang and Threadgill (2019) integrated this algorithm into CE-QUAL-W2 for the Columbia and Snake River hydropower facilities.

    The SYSTDG TDG regression equations are:

    No TDG Production Equation
    1 \(TDG_{sp} = P1 \cdot (1 - e^{P3 \cdot Q_{sp}}) + bp\)
    2 \(\Delta TGP_{sp} = P1 \cdot (1 - e^{P3 \cdot Q_{sp}})\); \(TDG_{sp} = P1 \cdot (twe - twce)^{P2} \cdot (1 - e^{P3 \cdot q_s}) + P4 + bp\)
    3 \(TDG_{sp} = P1 \cdot (twe - twce)^{P2} \cdot q_s^{P3} + P4 + bp\)
    4 \(TDG_{sp} = P1 \cdot (twe - twce) + P2 \cdot q_s^{P3} + P4 + bp\)
    5 \(TDG_{sp} = P1 \cdot (1 - e^{P2 \cdot q_s}) + P3 \cdot (Temp_{tw} - P4) + bp\)

    The flow weighted specific spillbay discharge is:

    \[ q_s = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{nb} Q_i^C}{\sum_{i=1}^{nb} Q_i^{(C-1)}} \tag{4-135} \]

    The average tailrace mixing TDG is:

    \[ TDG_{rel} = \frac{TDG_{sp}(Q_{sp} + Q_{ent}) + TDG_{ph}(Q_{ph} - Q_{ent})}{Q_{ph} + Q_{sp}} \tag{4-137} \]

    Dissolved Oxygen Saturation Computations¶

    The dissolved oxygen saturation is computed from Mortimer's (1981) formulation:

    \[ \Phi_{O2sat} = P_{alt} e^{(7.7117 - 1.31403[\ln(T+45.93)])} \tag{4-138} \]

    where:

    • \(T\) = water temperature, °C
    • \(P_{alt}\) = altitude correction factor = \(\left(1-\frac{H}{44.3}\right)^{5.25}\)
    • \(H\) = elevation of the waterbody, km above sea level

    The saturation value is also a function of the chlorinity in saline environments. The following equation is used to compute DO saturation for a saltwater waterbody type that includes salinity effects:

    \[ \Phi^*_{O2sat} = e^{\left(\ln(\Phi_{O2sat}) - S\left[1.7674 \times 10^{-2} - \frac{1.0754 \times 10^1}{T} + \frac{2.1407 \times 10^3}{T^2}\right]\right)} \tag{4-139} \]

    where:

    • \(S\) = salinity, kg m⁻³
    • \(T\) = temperature, K

    Sediments¶

    Organic sediment contributions to nutrients and dissolved oxygen demand are simulated using three methods.

    Zero-Order Model¶

    The zero-order process uses a specified sediment oxygen demand (SOD) and anoxic release rates for phosphorus, ammonium, inorganic carbon, and iron that are temperature dependent. Nutrient releases do not occur when dissolved oxygen concentrations are above a minimum value [O2LIM] nor do they occur if the SOD is set to zero. The sediment contribution to inorganic carbon is computed as a fraction of the sediment oxygen demand.

    First-Order Model¶

    The first-order sediment compartment requires specifying inclusion of this compartment in the simulation, a decay rate, and initial conditions. The sediment compartment is not transported, except for focusing of sediments to the bottom by a user-defined settling velocity. Instead, a compartmental equation is written for the sediment compartment that is solved in the subroutine SEDIMENT.

    The equation for the first-order sediment compartment is:

    \[ S_{sed} = \frac{\omega_{POMR}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{POMR} + \frac{\omega_{POML}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{POML} + \sum\frac{\omega_a A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_a - \gamma_{om}K_s\Phi_s + K_{epom}K_{eb}\Phi_e \]
    \[ - \frac{\omega_{SED}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_s + \frac{\omega_{CBOD}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{CBOD} - K_{burial}\Phi_s \tag{4-140} \]

    where:

    • \(\gamma_{OM}\) = rate multiplier for organic matter
    • \(\omega_{POM}\) = POM settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_a\) = algal settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_{CBOD}\) = particulate CBOD settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(\omega_{SED}\) = sediment settling velocity, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{sed}\) = sediment decay rate, s⁻¹
    • \(K_{burial}\) = sediment burial rate, s⁻¹
    • \(\Phi_a\) = algal concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{LPOM}\) = POM labile concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{RPOM}\) = POM refractory concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_s\) = organic sediment concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(Vol_{cell}\) = volume of computational cell, m³
    • \(A_{bottom}\) = area of bottom, m²
    • \(\Phi_e\) = epiphyton concentration, g m⁻³
    • \(K_{epom}\) = fraction of epiphyton that go to particulate fraction and settle into sediment at death
    • \(K_{em}\) = epiphyton mortality rate

    Sediment Variable Stoichiometry and Kinetics¶

    Variable stoichiometry of sediments is done automatically within the W2 model. In addition to sediment phosphorus and sediment nitrogen, there is also a sediment carbon compartment. The sediment carbon stoichiometry is variable because organic matter, algae, and periphyton/epiphyton may have differing carbon stoichiometry. The initial sediment stoichiometry for P, N, and C is based on the given initial stoichiometry for organic matter. The decay rate of sediment in a model cell is the mass-averaged decay rate of the LPOM, RPOM, and the CBOD groups.

    Sediment Phosphorus¶

    \[ S_{Psed} = \frac{\omega_{POMR}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{RPOM-P} + \frac{\omega_{POML}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{LPOM-P} + \sum\frac{\omega_a A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\delta_{Pa}\Phi_a - K_s\Phi_{S-P} \]
    \[ + \sum K_{epom}K_{em}\delta_{Pe}\Phi_e - \frac{\omega_{SED}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{S-P} \]

    Sediment Nitrogen¶

    \[ S_{Nsed} = \frac{\omega_{POMR}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{RPOM-N} + \frac{\omega_{POML}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{LPOM-N} + \sum\frac{\omega_a A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\delta_{Na}\Phi_a - K_s\Phi_{S-N} \]
    \[ + \sum K_{epom}K_{em}\delta_{Ne}\Phi_e - \frac{\omega_{SED}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{S-N} + \frac{\omega_{NO3-SED}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{NO3-N}f_{NO3-SED} \]

    where:

    • \(\omega_{NO3}\) = NO₃-N diffusion rate into sediments, m s⁻¹
    • \(f_{NO3-SED}\) = fraction of NO₃-N diffused into sediments that is incorporated into organic matter in the sediments (the rest, \(1-f_{NO3-SED}\), is denitrified into N₂)

    Sediment Carbon¶

    \[ S_{Csed} = \frac{\omega_{POMR}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\gamma_{COM}\Phi_{RPOM} + \frac{\omega_{POML}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\gamma_{COM}\Phi_{LPOM} + \sum\frac{\omega_a A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\delta_{Ca}\Phi_a - K_s\Phi_{S-C} \]
    \[ + \sum K_{epom}K_{em}\delta_{Ce}\Phi_e - \frac{\omega_{SED}A_{bottom}}{Vol_{cell}}\Phi_{S-C} \]

    Sediment Diagenesis Model¶

    The sediment diagenesis model in CE-QUAL-W2 Version 4 and later is based on work from ERM and Golder (2011), Prakash et al. (2015), and Berger and Wells (2014), supported by the Cumulative Environmental Management Association (CEMA), Canada.

    Sediment state variables include organic matter (labile, refractory, and inert), P, N, inorganic C, alkalinity, oxidized forms of Fe and Mn, reduced forms of Fe and Mn, CH₄, H₂S, sulfate, and temperature. There are also options in the sediment diagenesis model for bubble formation and organic sediment resuspension.

    Sediment Phosphorus (Diagenesis)¶

    Because the aerobic layer is assumed to be very thin, oxidation of particulate organic phosphorus is assumed to be negligible. The source/sink rate equation for phosphate in sediment layer 1 (aerobic) is assumed to be zero:

    \[ S_{PO41} = 0 \]

    The rate equation for phosphate in sediment layer 2 (anaerobic) is:

    \[ S_{PO42} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\underbrace{k_{POPi}\gamma_{POP}\Phi_{POPi}}_{\text{Diagenesis}} \]

    where three classes of particulate organic phosphorus (labile, refractory, and inert/slow refractory) are mineralized.

    Phosphate can exist in dissolved and particulate forms. Dissolved oxygen concentrations determine the extent to which dissolved phosphate sorbs to iron oxyhydroxide particulates. Within the aerobic layer phosphate sorbs to iron oxyhydroxide. At low dissolved oxygen concentrations, iron oxyhydroxide is reduced and the sorbed phosphate is released.

    Mass balance equations for layer 1 (aerobic) and layer 2 (anaerobic) are:

    \[ H_1\frac{d\Phi_{PO41}}{dt} = -K_{L01}(f_d\Phi_{PO41} - \Phi_{PO40}) + K_{L12}(f_d\Phi_{PO42} - f_d\Phi_{PO41}) + w_{12}(f_p\Phi_{PO42} - f_p\Phi_{PO41}) - w_2\Phi_{PO41} \]
    \[ H_2\frac{d\Phi_{PO42}}{dt} = -K_{L12}(\Phi_{PO42} - \Phi_{PO41}) - w_{12}(f_p\Phi_{PO42} - f_p\Phi_{PO41}) + w_2(\Phi_{PO41} - \Phi_{PO42}) + H_2 S_{PO42} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{PO41}\) = total phosphate concentration in layer 1, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{PO42}\) = total phosphate concentration in layer 2, g m⁻³
    • \(\Phi_{PO40}\) = phosphate concentration in water column, g m⁻³
    • \(K_{L01}\) = mass transfer coefficient between water column and layer 1, m s⁻¹
    • \(K_{L12}\) = mass transfer coefficient between layer 1 and layer 2, m s⁻¹
    • \(f_d\) = dissolved fraction of phosphate
    • \(f_p\) = particulate fraction of phosphate
    • \(w_{12}\) = particle mixing velocity between layer 1 and layer 2, m s⁻¹
    • \(w_2\) = burial velocity, m s⁻¹

    The fractions associated with dissolved and particulate forms can be calculated with (Chapra, 1997):

    \[ f_d = \left(\frac{1}{\varphi + K_{dp}(1-\varphi)\rho}\right) \]
    \[ f_p = \left(\frac{K_{dp}(1-\varphi)\rho}{\varphi + K_{dp}(1-\varphi)\rho}\right) \]

    where \(\varphi\) is sediment porosity, \(\rho\) is sediment density (g m⁻³), and \(K_{dp}\) is the phosphorus sorption coefficient (m³ g⁻¹).

    Consumption of DO Due to Sediment Resuspension¶

    Particulate organic matter in the sediments can be resuspended by turbulence caused by wind or by bottom scour caused by high current velocities. Once particulate organic matter has been resuspended into an aerobic water column, it will decay and consume dissolved oxygen.

    Wind-Induced Resuspension¶

    This algorithm for predicting the resuspension of particulate organic matter due to wind is based on work from Kang et al. (1982) where the bottom shear stress is computed based on wind speed, wind fetch, and depth.

    Bottom Scour Resuspension¶

    The CE-QUAL-W2 model can compute the bottom scour of sediment (inorganic or organic) using the scour velocity from Nielson (1992):

    \[ V_{scour} = \frac{0.00033\left(\frac{\theta'}{\theta_c}-1\right)(S_g-1)^{0.6}g^{0.6}d^{0.8}}{\nu} \]

    where \(\theta'\) is Shields' parameter, \(\theta_c\) is the critical Shields' parameter, \(S_g\) is specific gravity of solid, \(g\) is acceleration due to gravity, \(d\) is particle diameter, and \(\nu\) is molecular viscosity of water.

    Mass Balance Equations for Particulate Organic Matter¶

    The mass balance equation for POC in the anaerobic layer (layer 2) is:

    \[ H_2\frac{d\Phi_{POCi}}{dt} = -k_{POCi}\theta_{POCi}^{(T-20)}\Phi_{POCi}H_2 - w_2\Phi_{POCi} + f_{POCi}J_{POC} - E_{POCi} \]

    where:

    • \(\Phi_{POCi}\) = POC concentration in reactivity class \(i\) in layer 2, g m⁻³
    • \(J_{POC}\) = depositional flux of POC from water column, g m⁻² s⁻¹
    • \(E_{POCi}\) = resuspension rate of reactivity class \(i\), g m⁻² s⁻¹
    • \(k_{POCi}\) = reaction rate of reactivity class \(i\), s⁻¹
    • \(f_{POCi}\) = fraction of \(J_{POC}\) that is in reactivity class \(i\)
    • \(\theta_{POCi}\) = temperature coefficient for reactivity class \(i\)
    • \(w_2\) = burial velocity, m s⁻¹

    Dynamic Calculation of Sediment pH and Temperature¶

    pH¶

    The dynamic calculation of pH was implemented using a pH model similar to one developed by the United States Geological Survey (Sullivan et al., 2013). In order to calculate pH, sediment organic carbon, sediment alkalinity, and sediment temperature also had to be modeled.

    Sediment Total Inorganic Carbon¶

    The rate equation for TIC in the aerobic sediment layer 1 is:

    \[ S_{TIC1} = \underbrace{\sum k_{POC1}\gamma_{POC}\Phi_{POC}}_{\text{POC decay}} + \underbrace{k_{CH41}\gamma_{CH4}\Phi_{CH4}}_{\text{CH₄ decay}} \]

    The rate equation for TIC in the anaerobic sediment layer 2 is:

    \[ S_{TIC2} = \underbrace{\sum k_{POCm2}\gamma_{POC}\Phi_{POC}}_{\text{Methanogenesis}} + \underbrace{k_{POCs2}\gamma_{POC}\Phi_{POC}}_{\text{Sulfide Diagenesis}} \]

    The mass transfer coefficient \(K_{L12}\) is related to the diffusion coefficient \(D\) using:

    \[ K_{L12} = \frac{D}{\Delta z} = \frac{D}{\frac{1}{2}(H_1 + H_2)} \]

    Sediment Alkalinity¶

    Sources and sinks for non-conservative sediment alkalinity include nitrification (decreases alkalinity) and denitrification (increases alkalinity). Alkalinity is in units of g m⁻³ as CaCO₃.

    The rate equation for alkalinity in the aerobic sediment layer 1 is:

    \[ S_{ALK1} = -\underbrace{\left(\frac{2 \text{ equivalents alkalinity}}{1 \text{ mole ammonium nitrified}}\right)k_{NH4}\gamma_{NH4}\Phi_{NH4}}_{\text{nitrification}} + \underbrace{\left(\frac{1 \text{ equivalent alkalinity}}{1 \text{ mole nitrate denitrified}}\right)k_{NOx}\gamma_{NOx1}\Phi_{NOx}}_{\text{denitrification}} \]

    The rate equation for alkalinity in the anaerobic sediment layer 2 is:

    \[ S_{ALK2} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{1 \text{ equivalent alkalinity}}{1 \text{ mole nitrate denitrified}}\right)k_{NOx}\gamma_{NOx}\Phi_{NOx}}_{\text{denitrification}} \]

    Sediment Temperature¶

    The heat balances for the aerobic layer 1 and anaerobic layer 2 are:

    \[ \rho C_p H_1 \frac{dT_1}{dt} = -K_{L01}\rho C_p(T_1 - T_0) + K_{L12}\rho C_p(T_2 - T_1) \]
    \[ \rho C_p H_2 \frac{dT_2}{dt} = -K_{L12}\rho C_p(T_2 - T_1) + K_{sw}(T_{sed} - T_2) \]

    where:

    • \(T_1, T_2\) = temperature in layer 1 and layer 2, °C
    • \(T_0\) = temperature in water column, °C
    • \(T_{sed}\) = temperature of sediments below anaerobic layer, °C
    • \(K_{sw}\) = coefficient of sediment heat exchange between anaerobic layer and sediments below, W m⁻² °C⁻¹

    Metal (Fe and Mn) Complexation and Diagenesis¶

    Ferrous Iron Fe(II)¶

    Iron is modeled using two constituents: iron oxyhydroxide, FeOOH(s), and ferrous iron Fe(II). FeOOH(s) is very insoluble and is modeled as a particulate whereas Fe(II) is more soluble.

    The rate equation for ferrous iron in sediment layer 1 (aerobic) is:

    \[ S_{Fe(II)1} = -\underbrace{k_{Fe(II)1}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH1-7)}f_d\Phi_{Fe(II)1}}_{\text{Oxidation}} \]

    The rate equation for ferrous iron in sediment layer 2 (anaerobic) is:

    \[ S_{Fe(II)2} = \underbrace{k_{FeOOH2}\Phi_{FeOOH}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    Note that if pH < 4, the term \(10^{2(pH1-7)}\) was set to 1E-6, and if pH > 8, \(10^{2(pH1-7)}\) was set to 1E2.

    All kinetic rates are adjusted by temperature using: \(k_T = k_{20}\theta^{T-20}\) where \(\theta\) = 1.05.

    Iron Oxyhydroxide FeOOH(s)¶

    The rate equation for iron oxyhydroxide in the aerobic sediment layer 1 is:

    \[ S_{FeOOH1} = \underbrace{k_{Fe(II)1}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH1-7)}f_d\Phi_{Fe(II)1}}_{\text{Oxidation}} \]

    The rate equation for iron oxyhydroxide in the anaerobic sediment layer 2 is:

    \[ S_{FeOOH2} = -\underbrace{k_{FeOOH2}\Phi_{FeOOH}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    Manganese Mn(II)¶

    The manganese model within the water column and sediments is analogous to the iron model.

    The rate equation for Mn(II) in the water column is:

    \[ S_{Mn(II)} = -\underbrace{k_{Mn(II)0}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH-7)}\Phi_{Mn(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} + \underbrace{k_{MnO_2}\left(\frac{K_{MnO_2}}{K_{MnO_2}+\Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{MnO_2}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    The rate equation for Mn(II) in the aerobic sediment layer 1 is:

    \[ S_{Mn(II)1} = -\underbrace{k_{Mn(II)1}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH1-7)}f_d\Phi_{Mn(II)1}}_{\text{Oxidation}} \]

    Manganese Dioxide MnO₂¶

    The rate equation for manganese dioxide in the water column is:

    \[ S_{MnO_2} = \underbrace{k_{Mn(II)0}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH-7)}\Phi_{Mn(II)}}_{\text{Oxidation}} - \underbrace{k_{MnO_2}\left(\frac{K_{MnO_2}}{K_{MnO_2}+\Phi_{DO}}\right)\Phi_{MnO_2}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    The rate equation for MnO₂ in the aerobic sediment layer 1 is:

    \[ S_{MnO_21} = \underbrace{k_{Mn(II)1}\Phi_{DO}10^{2(pH1-7)}f_d\Phi_{Mn(II)1}}_{\text{Oxidation}} \]

    The rate equation for MnO₂ in the anaerobic sediment layer 2 is:

    \[ S_{MnO_22} = -\underbrace{k_{MnO_22}\Phi_{MnO_2}}_{\text{Reduction}} \]

    Total Inorganic Carbon¶

    Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are the most abundant elements in living matter and form the essential backbone of organic material. Inorganic carbon directly influences pH.

    The rate equation for total inorganic carbon is:

    \[ S_{TIC} = \underbrace{\sum(K_{ar}-K_{ag})\delta_{Ca}\Phi_a}_{\text{net algal production}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{er}-K_{eg})\delta_{Ce}\Phi_e}_{\text{net epiphyton production}} + \underbrace{K_{LPOM}\delta_{COM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{LPOM}}_{\text{labile POM decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{LDOM}\gamma_{OM}\delta_{COM}\Phi_{LDOM}}_{\text{labile DOM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{RDOM}\gamma_{OM}\delta_{COM}\Phi_{RDOM}}_{\text{refractory DOM decay}} + \underbrace{K_{RPOM}\delta_{COM}\gamma_{OM}\Phi_{RPOM}}_{\text{refractory POM decay}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{K_{sed}\gamma_{OM}\delta_{COM}\Phi_{sed}}_{\text{1st-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{SOD\gamma_{OM}\delta_{COM}\frac{A_{sed}}{V}}_{\text{0-order sediment release}} + \underbrace{\frac{A_{sur}}{V_{sur}}K_{Lc}(\Phi_{sCO2} - \Phi_{CO2})}_{\text{reaeration}} \]
    \[ + \underbrace{\sum K_{CBOD}R_{CBOD}\delta_{C-CBOD}\Theta^{T-20}\Phi_{CBOD}}_{\text{CBOD decay}} + \underbrace{\sum(K_{mr}-K_{mg})\delta_{Cm}\Phi_{macro}}_{\text{net macrophyte production}} + \underbrace{K_{zr}\delta_{Czoo}\gamma_{zoo}\Phi_{zoo}}_{\text{zooplankton respiration}} \tag{4-141} \]

    TIC is in units of g m⁻³ as C.

    CO₂ Gas Transfer¶

    The gas transfer coefficient for CO₂ is related to that of oxygen by:

    \[ KL_{CO2} = KL_{O2}\sqrt{\frac{D_{CO2}}{D_{O2}}} \tag{4-142} \]

    Using the fact that the ratio of molecular diffusion coefficients of two gases A and B are related to their molecular weights:

    \[ \frac{D_A}{D_B} = \sqrt{\frac{MW_B}{MW_A}} \tag{4-143} \]

    then:

    \[ KL_{CO2} = KL_{O2}\left(\frac{MW_{O2}}{MW_{CO2}}\right)^{0.25} = 0.923 KL_{O2} \tag{4-144} \]

    CO₂ Saturation¶

    Since Version 4.2.2, the CO₂ saturation was determined from:

    \[ \Phi'_{CO2} = K_H P_{CO2} \tag{4-146} \]

    where \(K_H\) is the temperature-dependent Henry's Law constant in moles CO₂/l/atm and \(P_{CO2}\) is the partial pressure of CO₂ in atm. \(K_H\) is determined from Edmond and Gieskes (1970):

    \[ K_H = 10^{\left(\frac{2385.73}{TK} + 0.0152642TK - 14.0184\right)} \tag{4-147} \]

    where \(TK\) is the water temperature in K. Converting to CO₂ saturation as C in g C m⁻³:

    \[ \Phi'_{CO2} = 12000 \times 10^{\left(\frac{2385.73}{TK} + 0.0152642TK - 14.0184\right)} P_{CO2} \tag{4-148} \]

    Computation of \(P_{CO2}\) in atm is based on:

    \[ P_{CO2} = P_{CO2\_ppm} P_a \tag{4-149} \]

    where \(P_{CO2\_ppm}\) is the ppm of CO₂ in the atmosphere and \(P_a\) is the altitude correction or the atmospheric pressure in atm:

    \[ P_a = \left(1 - \frac{H}{44.3}\right)^{5.25} \tag{4-150} \]

    where \(H\) is the reservoir elevation from sea level in km.

    Alkalinity¶

    Alkalinity is in units of g m⁻³ as CaCO₃ and provides an indication of the buffering capacity of aquatic systems and their resistance to pH changes from acidic or alkaline loadings. Alkalinity, along with total inorganic carbon, is used to determine pH and concentrations of carbonate species in subroutine PH_CO2.

    Alkalinity can be treated as conservative in the model with the internal rate term set to zero. However, alkalinity variations are common in aquatic systems. Whiting (large precipitations of carbonates) may occur, and sediment release of carbonates may increase alkalinity in the anoxic zones of many reservoirs. If these effects are significant, use the non-conservative alkalinity model.

    A non-conservative alkalinity algorithm based on Sullivan et al. (2013) and Stumm and Morgan (1996) accounts for alkalinity variations over time. This algorithm includes the utilization of ammonia and nitrate during photosynthesis, production of ammonia during respiration, nitrification, and denitrification.

    pH and Carbonate Species¶

    The pH and carbonate species are computed using water temperature, TDS or salinity, alkalinity, and total inorganic carbon concentrations using basic carbonate relationships. Computed carbonate species are carbon dioxide, bicarbonates, and carbonates. The pH and carbonate species are computed in subroutine PH_CO2. These materials are not subject to transport and are computed at each water quality update interval.

    Calculations performed by subroutine PH_CO2 are based on the carbonate-bicarbonate equilibrium reaction (Stumm and Morgan 1996):

    \[ \text{HCO}_3^- \leftrightarrow \text{CO}_3^{2-} + \text{H}^+ \tag{4-151} \]
    \[ \text{H}_2\text{O} \leftrightarrow \text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \tag{4-152} \]

    The equilibrium state in terms of the equilibrium constants \(K_i\) is:

    \[ K_1 = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{HCO}_3^-]}{[\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3]} \tag{4-153} \]
    \[ K_2 = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{CO}_3^{2-}]}{[\text{HCO}_3^-]} \tag{4-154} \]
    \[ K_w = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{H}_2\text{O}]} \tag{4-155} \]

    where \([X]\) is molar concentration (moles liter⁻¹) and \([\text{H}_2\text{O}]\) = unity (by definition).

    Alkalinity is defined as:

    \[ [\text{ALK}] = [\text{HCO}_3^-] + 2[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] + [\text{OH}^-] - [\text{H}^+] \tag{4-156} \]

    The following condition prevails for the dissolution of carbonic acid:

    \[ [\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3] + [\text{HCO}_3^-] + [\text{CO}_3^{2-}] = \text{CONSTANT} = C_T \tag{4-157} \]

    By combining these equations, the quantities \([\text{HCO}_3^-]\) and \([\text{CO}_3^{2-}]\) can be expressed in terms of \([\text{H}^+]\) and the constant \(C_T\). Substituting into the alkalinity expression:

    \[ [\text{ALK}] = \frac{C_T[\text{H}^+]K_1}{[\text{H}^+]K_1 + K_1K_2 + [\text{H}^+]^2} + \frac{[\text{H}^+] + 2K_2}{[\text{H}^+]} + \frac{K_w}{[\text{H}^+]} - [\text{H}^+] \tag{4-158} \]

    The model interprets the constant \(C_T\) as moles per liter of total inorganic carbon and assumes \([\text{ALK}]\) is known. This equation is iteratively solved in subroutine PH_CO2 until the value of \([\text{H}^+]\) converges. The negative logarithm of \([\text{H}^+]\) is, by definition, pH.

    Once \([\text{H}^+]\) has been solved for, the carbonate species are computed:

    \[ [\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3] = \frac{C_T}{1 + \frac{K_1}{[\text{H}^+]} + \frac{K_1K_2}{[\text{H}^+]^2}} \tag{4-159} \]
    \[ [\text{HCO}_3^-] = \frac{C_T}{1 + \frac{[\text{H}^+]}{K_1} + \frac{K_2}{[\text{H}^+]}} \tag{4-160} \]
    \[ [\text{CO}_3^{2-}] = \frac{C_T}{1 + \frac{[\text{H}^+]^2}{K_1K_2} + \frac{[\text{H}^+]}{K_2}} \tag{4-161} \]

    which are then converted to grams per cubic meter.

    Equilibrium Constants¶

    Equilibrium constants are obtained by first expressing a thermodynamic temperature dependence for a related constant, \(K_i^*\):

    \[ \log K_i^* = a + \frac{b}{T} + cT + d\log T \tag{4-162} \]

    The constants \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), and \(d\) are:

    a b c d
    \(K_w^*\) 35.3944 -5242.39 -0.00835 -11.8261
    \(K_1^*\) 14.8435 -3404.71 -0.03278 0
    \(K_2^*\) 6.4980 -2902.39 -0.02379 0

    Activity Coefficients¶

    Activity coefficients are obtained from an extension of Debye-Hückel theory:

    \[ \log \gamma = \frac{-AZ^2\sqrt{I}}{1 + 0.33a\sqrt{I}} + k_1 + k_2 I + k_3 I^2 \tag{4-168} \]

    where:

    • \(I\) = ionic strength
    • \(Z\) = ionic charge
    • \(A\) = approximately 0.5 for water at 25 °C
    • \(a\) = ionic size parameter
    • \(k_i\) = empirical coefficients

    Ionic strength based on the concentration of total dissolved solids (TDS) in g m⁻³ is approximated as:

    \[ I = 2.5 \times 10^5 \Phi_{TDS} \tag{4-169} \]

    or for salinity in units of kg m⁻³:

    \[ I = 0.00147 + 0.019885\Phi_{salinity} + 0.000038\Phi_{salinity}^2 \tag{4-170} \]

    Activity coefficients for special cases:

    \[ [\text{H}^+]\gamma = [\text{H}_2\text{O}]\gamma = 1 \tag{4-171} \]
    \[ [\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3]\gamma = [\text{OH}^-]\gamma = 0.0755I \tag{4-172} \]

    Temperature Rate Multipliers¶

    Most biological and chemical rates are temperature dependent. Subroutine RATE_MULTIPLIERS calculates the temperature dependence for all rates. It is called after the temperature solution so the temperature of the current computational interval is used.

    The temperature rate multiplier function (Thornton and Lessem, 1978) has an optimum range and diminishes asymmetrically at higher and lower temperatures:

    \[ \lambda_T = 0 \quad \text{where } T \leq T_1 \]
    \[ \lambda_T = \frac{K_1 e^{\gamma_{ar}(T-T_1)}}{1 + K_1 e^{\gamma_{ar}(T-T_1)} - K_1} \cdot \frac{K_4 e^{\gamma_{af}(T_4-T)}}{1 + K_4 e^{\gamma_{af}(T_4-T)} - K_4} \quad \text{where } T_1 < T < T_4 \tag{4-173} \]
    \[ \lambda_T = 0 \quad \text{where } T \geq T_4 \]

    where:

    \[ \gamma_{ar} = \frac{1}{T_2 - T_1}\ln\frac{K_2(1-K_1)}{K_1(1-K_2)} \]
    \[ \gamma_{af} = \frac{1}{T_4 - T_3}\ln\frac{K_3(1-K_4)}{K_4(1-K_3)} \]

    \(\gamma_{ar}\) and \(\gamma_{af}\) are the rising and falling limb temperature multipliers.

    The user supplies temperatures \(T_1\) to \(T_4\) and multiplier factors \(K_1\) to \(K_4\). Temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_4\) represent mortality limits, and \(T_2\) and \(T_3\) are used to define the optimum range. Maximum reaction rates supplied by the user are multiplied by \(\lambda_T\) to determine rates corresponding to the water temperature of a model cell. For non-algae temperature rate multipliers, only the \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) corresponding to \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are used.

    Note

    For generic constituents and CBOD, Arrhenius temperature coefficients of the form \(k_{T1} = k_{T2}\theta^{T1-T2}\) may be used, where \(k_T\) is the kinetic first-order rate constant in day⁻¹ at temperature \(T\) in °C and \(\theta\) is an empirical coefficient dependent on the reaction.
